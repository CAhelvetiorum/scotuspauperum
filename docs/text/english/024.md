{#s024-h .heading} [Ninth question.]

{#s024-01} The ninth question of the third distinction, which asks whether the intellective part properly so called, or something of it, is the total cause begetting actual knowledge, or the ground of begetting it: that is, whether the intellect is the total and active cause of the act of understanding, or whether the object is required together with the intellect. It seems that it is: for fruitful memory is the parent of begotten knowledge according to Augustine; but fruitful memory is precisely the intellective power itself; therefore etc. To the contrary is Augustine, *On the Trinity* IX.

{#s024-02} At the beginning of the solution the solution of Henry is set out, *Quodlibet* IV q. 7; and afterwards, where the Doctor says “For this opinion one argues by reasons,” it is the same Henry, *Quodlibet* V q. 5. He attributes the whole activity with respect to intellection to the soul itself. The second opinion is set out where the Doctor says “There is another opinion, which is entirely at the other extreme.” It is the opinion of Godfrey of Fontaines, *Quodlibet* VI q. 7, *Quodlibet* VIII q. 2, *Quodlibet* IX q. 19 and *Quodlibet* XIII q. 3; and this opinion attributes the whole activity to the object. “The third opinion,” says the Doctor, etc.: this is Henry’s, *Quodlibet* V q. 14, and he says that the intellect is active as it reasons discursively, passive as it receives. And afterwards, where the Doctor says “This same position about passion” etc., it is Henry, *Quodlibet* III q. 6; and this is the fourth opinion.

{#s024-03} The fifth opinion is set out where the Doctor says “The fifth opinion holds that the species of the object in the intellect” etc. It is Thomas’s, *Summa* I q. 85 a. 2. He says that the species of the object in the intellect, or the object itself present in itself, is what begets, or is the formal ground of begetting, actual knowledge in the intellect; and the intellect itself behaves only as the material element there, informed by that species, or as having the object that takes the place of the species. The sixth opinion is at “an opinion which comes back to the same”; it is Giles’s, *Quodlibet* III qq. 12, 13, 14. And where he says “These two opinions, however, namely the third and the fourth” etc., this is from Henry’s *Quodlibet* IV q. 8. And where the Doctor afterwards argues against this kind of harmonization, at the place where he says “This is confirmed according to the opinion of that doctor” etc., it is Henry, [[p. 40|v° · D.iii. Q.ix]] *Quodlibet* V q. 19. Further, in replying afterwards to the arguments of Thomas in that place: “But that authority which says that the possible intellect” etc., “which some treat as necessary” — this is Thomas, *Summa* I q. 87 a. 1, and Giles of Rome, *Quodlibet* I q. 3 and *Quodlibet* II q. 23. Of these opinions, some say that intellection comes from the intellect alone, others from the object alone, others that the intellect behaves as the material element.

{#s024-04} But coming to Scotus’s opinion, it is said that actual intellection is something in us that is not perpetual but has being after non-being; and that the soul and the present object must concur, and that in an intelligible species, since otherwise [the object] is neither present nor actually intelligible, speaking of a sensible and material object. And so the object is not the total cause, neither in itself nor in its intelligible species; nor is the intellect, according to Augustine, who says that knowledge is brought forth from the knower and the known. From this authority it is had that object and intellect are required for intellection.

{#s024-05} But from that authority the Nominalists wish to conclude that no species is required for the act of understanding; and they do not speak rightly. For the authority does not say that [knowledge comes] from the object and the power only, but that object and power are required, and with this something else, namely the species (*On the Soul* III). They also adduce the text of Aristotle, *Ethics* II, namely that there are three things in the soul: affections, powers, and habits; and from this they conclude: therefore not species. But that text is not adduced to the purpose. For Aristotle’s intention is that there are three things in the soul which are principles of doing; and so his intention is other than the intention for which they adduce the text.

{#s024-06} But as for one authority adduced in the fifth opinion, where it is said that the intellect behaves as matter because (*On the Soul* III) understanding is in a certain way being acted upon: Scotus says that the Philosopher means the powers of the soul by which we are formally in second act, for instance the sense, inasmuch as it is that by which we formally sense, and the intellect, by which we formally understand. And we formally understand by the intellect inasmuch as it receives intellection. By these words they wish to say that Scotus’s intention is that understanding is receiving intellection. Next the Doctor says that Aristotle did not say that the possible intellect does not act in understanding. For [if] Scotus says “Aristotle did not say that the intellect does not act in understanding; therefore it does not act,” the consequence is invalid, [being] from authority negatively. And so it is Scotus’s intention, *Quodlibet* 15, that the possible intellect acts in understanding.

{#s024-07} And therefore a certain [doctor] says thus, and well: the possible intellect has these two [functions], namely to receive the species [[p. 41|r° · D.iii. Q.x]] and to go out into the act of understanding. In the first, that is, in the reception of the species, it is passive; in the second, that is, when it goes out into the act of understanding, it is active. And therefore Aristotle said (*On the Soul* III) that understanding is in a certain way being acted upon. He did not say “being acted upon” simply, but “in a certain way,” that is, as regards reception. That it behaves actively when it goes out into act can be shown by Aristotle, *Ethics* VI, who says that understanding is an immanent action; an action is in the agent (*Physics* III); but understanding, which is an action, is in the possible intellect; therefore the possible [intellect] is an agent. Otherwise no greater action would be attributed to the possible intellect than to prime matter, which no philosopher would say.

{#s024-08} And note that below, at the arguments of the first opinion, you will find in what way this authority is true: “the agent is more excellent than the patient.” Speaking absolutely and simply, it is false: for the sun acts upon a man, yet it is not nobler than a man, since living things are better than non-living according to Augustine. But it is to be understood [thus]: the agent as agent is more excellent than the patient; and this is true in all cases. To the argument at the beginning, I say that fruitful memory includes the intellective power together with the object, wherefore etc.
