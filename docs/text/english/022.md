{#s022-h .heading} [Seventh question.]

{#s022-01} The seventh question of the third distinction, which asks whether memory in the intellective [part] preserves the intelligible species once the act of understanding has ceased. It seems not: if it preserved one species, by the same reasoning it would preserve another species [[p. 38|v° · Di.iii. Q.viii]] of another object, and so infinitely many at once. To the contrary is Aristotle, *On the Soul* III. Again, the Commentator calls memory the storehouse of species.

{#s022-02} In this question the opinion of Avicenna is set out, who says that a species must be posited. But some interpret Avicenna as having understood that there is a twofold species. One is abstracted from sensible things, and this remains when the act of the intellect passes. The other is had from the influx of the Intelligence, to which the intellect turns, and this does not remain when the act of the intellect does not remain.

{#s022-03} But the Doctor argues against those who hold that the species does not remain, and so he says to the question that the species remains. For there is the text of *On the Soul* III: the intellect, insofar as it is intellect, can be wise of itself. But of itself it would not be wise about any object except insofar as the object were present to it under the aspect of object; and this belongs to it only through an intelligible species. This is had from Aristotle, *On the Soul* III, who says that memory is the storehouse of species. And so, through species preserved in memory, the intellect can go out into the act of understanding without a phantasm. And if it is said that Aristotle, *On the Soul* III, says that “one who understands must contemplate phantasms,” it is said that this is to be understood [as] ‘or to have contemplated [them]’. For it is not necessary always to have recourse to phantasms: [the intellect] can understand without a phantasm through species preserved in memory. And so it is necessary to posit the intellective part as preserving intelligible species. To the argument at the beginning it is said that it would not contain infinitely many species, but finitely many, [increasing] without limit, which is not unfitting; or infinitely many in a qualified sense.
