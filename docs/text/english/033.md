{#s033-h .heading} [Eighth distinction. First question.]

{#s033-01} On the eighth distinction it is asked whether God is supremely simple. It seems that he is not simple; for he is wise, [and] wisdom in us is an accident; therefore [also] in God. This is plain from *Physics* I: for what truly is, is accidental to nothing. To the contrary is Augustine, *On the Trinity* VI, chapter 6: “God is supremely simple.”

{#s033-02} In this question there are no opinions; so to the question it is said that he is, on the authority of Augustine, *On the Trinity* V, who says that the Holy Spirit is equal to the Father and the Son through supreme simplicity. But for fuller explanation four things will be done in this question. First, the question will be answered by way of certain statements. Second, [it will be asked] whether non-identity or distinction can exist together with supreme simplicity. Third, whether there is distinction together with supreme identity. Fourth, how many modes of identity there are in God.

{#s033-03} To the first it is said that the simple is what cannot be resolved into anything else of which it is composed, but at which the ultimate resolution stands, yet which can be composed with another, as being. But the supremely simple is what is not resolvable, but at which the ultimate resolution stops, and which is neither composable with another nor can be composed; and this is found only in God. The simply simple is found in creatures, as being; for otherwise, if there were no terminus, there would be a process to infinity. But in creatures one cannot find the supremely simple, because the things that are in a creature are composable. Then, to the question whether God is supremely simple, it is said that he is simple and supremely simple.

{#s033-04} To the second it is said that non-identity or distinction without composition is compatible with supreme simplicity. That [it is] without composition is plain: for there is no composition of matter and form, nor of subject and accident, nor of [[p. 55|r° · Di.viii. Q.i]] genus and difference; for then [God] would have to be in a genus, which is false, since he is unlimited. That there is non-identity without composition is plain: for Father, Son and Holy Spirit in the divine essence are not the same, and they do not make a composition. That things are distinct together with supreme simplicity is plain: for the human and the divine nature are distinct, as finite and infinite, yet there they stand together with supreme simplicity; or [again], the three persons are distinct and stand together with supreme simplicity in the essence; therefore etc.

{#s033-05} To the third it is said that distinction is not compatible with supreme identity, because things that are supremely the same are not distinguished. And if it is said, “all things in God are supreme; therefore if they are distinguished, they are supremely distinguished”, the consequence does not hold, because the supreme in God is [the supreme] of perfection [only]; for otherwise the persons in God would be supremely the same and not supremely the same.

{#s033-06} To the fourth it is said that there are eight modes of identity. The first is between paternity and filiation, because they are the same essentially and are distinguished really. The second mode of identity is greater, namely that of the persons themselves as already constituted, that is, of Father, Son and Holy Spirit: these are the same essentially and yet differ really; and these are more the same than in the first mode, because the persons include the essence, whereas the properties [only] are compared and referred to the essence. The third mode is that of active generation and active spiration; and these are more the same than in the previous modes, because they are in the same person. The fourth mode is that of the essence and the relative properties, which are the same really but not formally; and this is a greater identity, because there is only one infinite there, namely the essence; in the others not. The fifth mode is that of the attributes themselves, namely wisdom and goodness; and it is greater, because they are the same essentially and really, and each is infinite, whereas in the preceding [mode] there was only one infinite. The sixth mode is that of the attributes compared to the divine essence itself; and this is a greater identity, for there is greater identity of a property with its subject than of a property with a property. The seventh mode is that of the essence with its intrinsic degrees; and this is greater, because they are the same really, essentially and formally, but differ by the nature of the thing. The eighth mode is that of the essence itself with its supreme being; and this is the same in every way, and here [the series] stops.

{#s033-07} Note, at the beginning of the question: “wisdom in God is substance; therefore in us it is not an accident”, as is plain from *Physics* I, “what truly is, is accidental to nothing.” It is said that this is true of that which truly is; but in God [wisdom] does not [so] truly “be”: for the word “is” must be taken in the third mode of saying *per se*, that is, what has *per se* a solitary and separate being, which is not so with wisdom. [[p. 56|v° · Di.viii. Q.ii]] In the first reply note what a pure perfection is. It is said that a pure perfection is that which it is better to be than not to be it, that is, [better than] anything incompossible with it. To the argument at the beginning I say that wisdom as a species of quality is not of the same notion in us and in God.
