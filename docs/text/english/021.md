{#s021-h .heading} [Sixth question.]

{#s021-01} The sixth question of the third distinction, in which the image is inquired into. He asks first about memory, namely whether in the intellective part properly taken there is a memory having an intelligible species. It seems not: what is begotten is assimilated to what begets it; but when a species is impressed by an object, it is impressed by it as a singular, because action belongs to singulars; therefore the impressed species cannot represent the universal, such as is required to move the intellect. To the contrary is Aristotle, *On the Soul* III, who posits species.

{#s021-02} Immediately at the beginning of the solution the opinion of Henry, *Quodlibet* IV q. 8, is set out, and above all that of Richard, *Quodlibet* V q. 14. A little later, where he says “Another doctor holds this,” this other is Godfrey of Fontaines, *Quodlibet* IX q. 19 and *Quodlibet* XIII q. 3. These men say that no intelligible species is [[p. 37|r° · D.iii. Q.vii]] to be posited. The nominalists are of this mind.

{#s021-03} But coming to the point of the question, the opinion of Scotus and of those who understand Aristotle soundly is that an intelligible species is to be posited. For the Philosopher says in *On the Soul* III: “The stone is not in the soul, but the species of the stone.” Again, Augustine, *On the Trinity* XI, says that there are four species in the soul, each born of the other: from the species born in the sensitive [power] comes the species in the phantasy; from the one in the phantasy, the one in the memory; and from the one in the memory, the act of understanding. This could also be confirmed from the evening knowledge of the angels, where species must be posited. Again, the soul of Christ knew many things whose presence it did not have, and so [it knew them] through species. Again, separated souls will remember through species. Again, by what did Paul remember the things he had seen in his rapture? Certainly not by habits, as the nominalists say, for he could not have them from a single act, according to Aristotle, *Ethics* II. Therefore through species.

{#s021-04} But granted that a species is posited, how does it stand: is it the total cause, or an instrument, or a cause *sine qua non*? It is said: neither this nor that, but a particular cause determining the universal cause, of which the universal cause is the intellect. For it determines the intellect so that it understands Peter and not John.

{#s021-05} But someone might ask how, since the species is singular, it can represent the quiddity and move the intellect to a concept of it. I say that in the species two things are to be considered, namely the nature and the haecceity. It is said, then, that the formal principle of acting is the quiddity of the species, while the haecceity is a condition of it. The species therefore moves by reason of its quiddity, which quiddity is immaterial, and so is the intellect; and thus there is proportion between mover and moved (*On the Soul* III). It does not, then, move by reason of that property or haecceity, but by reason of the quiddity or nature. From what has been said, the answer to the question is that a memory must necessarily be posited in the intellective part. To the argument at the beginning it is said that the species does not move under the aspect of the singular, but under the aspect of the universal and incorruptible.
