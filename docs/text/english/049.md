{#s049-h .heading} [Third question.]

{#s049-01} The third question of the fifteenth distinction, where it is asked whether any relation whatever can found a relation. On the question it is said that necessity constitutes the Son, according to Hilary; but necessity is a relation.

{#s049-02} But for a fuller clarification four things will be considered. First, [[p. 80|v° · Di.xv. Q.iii]] whether a relation can immediately found a substance. Second, whether a relation can immediately found a relation. Third, whether passive spiration can found a relation. Fourth, whether a relation is defined by something of its own genus.

{#s049-03} As to the first, the holy Doctor, *Prima Pars*, question 39, article 2, says no. But the Doctor thinks otherwise. For if a relation could not found a substance, it would follow that matter and form were not the cause of accidents. But they are the cause; therefore, etc. The major is clear: causality is a certain relation, and this causality is founded immediately on matter and form, and so immediately in a substance. The minor is the Philosopher's, *Physics* 1, that matter and form are the cause of all the accidents existing in them. And so some relations, though not all, can found a substance.

{#s049-04} As to the second, it is said that a relation can be founded on a relation, because proportionality can be founded on proportion, and likeness on equality. But these are relatives; therefore a relation is founded on a relation.

{#s049-05} As to the third, whether passive spiration can found a relation, it is said that it can. For a divine relation founds a [fourfold] relation. The first relation it founds is the relation of distinction: for paternity is distinguished from the essence, taking *distinction* in a broad sense. The second is the relation of identity, because paternity is really the same as the essence. The third is the relation of origin, because paternity has an order of origin with the essence itself, according to Dionysius; and the properties in God have an order to one another. The fourth is constitution, because paternity constitutes the Father.

{#s049-06} As to the fourth, whether a relation is defined by something of its own genus, it is said that although, in common usage, a relation is defined by its opposite, this is not the proper definition of a relation. For a relation ought to be defined by its genus and its difference. Therefore, if we say “what is a father?”, we ought to say: a father is a relation ⟨…⟩, and we shall not say “one who has a son or a daughter”, since son and daughter are neither genus nor difference. But is passive spiration ⟨definable⟩? It is said not, for it is supremely simple and transcendent; and although it has a description, some nevertheless say that it has no definition at all.
