{#s032-h .heading} [Seventh distinction. First question.]

{#s032-01} On the seventh distinction it is asked whether the power of begetting in the Father is something absolute or relative. It seems that the power is not something absolute; for Augustine says in *On the Trinity* XV, “for he is Father by that by which [there] is a Son”; therefore by that by which [[p. 53|r° · Di.vii. Q.i]] he is Father, he begets. But he is Father by paternity; therefore he begets by paternity. To the contrary is Damascene, book I, chapter 8: “Generation is a work of nature”; but nature is something absolute; therefore etc.

{#s032-02} At the end of the arguments at the foot of the question the opinion of Thomas is set out, *Summa*, first part, question 40, article 5, who says that the Father begets by the essence. Afterwards, where the Doctor says, “it is said otherwise, that for generation in God one must posit etc.”, this is the opinion of Henry, *Quodlibet* III, question 5, and in the *Summa*, article 57, question 7. These opinions having been set out (the first, that of Thomas, saying that that by which the Father begets is the essence; the second, Henry’s, that the essence is the eliciting principle of generation and the relation the determining principle),

{#s032-03} there follows the opinion of the Doctor, who draws a distinction: “power” is taken in several ways. In one way, for logical power, which signifies the non-repugnance of terms, as is plain from *Metaphysics* V. In another way power is taken as it is distinguished from act; and in this sense it is said in *Metaphysics* IV that act and potency divide being. In this way the same thing is in potency before it is in act; and such potency is not in God, since God is pure act. In another way power is taken as it is a principle of acting and of being acted upon. Taking power as it is a principle of being acted upon, it is not posited in God, because power in that sense implies imperfection. But taking power as it is a principle of acting, it can be taken in two ways: in one way, as to what it signifies *per se*; in another, as to what it denominates *per se*. Speaking of power as to what it signifies *per se*, I say that it signifies a relation. But speaking of power as to what it denominates *per se*, that is, speaking of the proximate foundation of that relation, all concurrent [factors] whatever being strictly set aside, the Doctor then answers the question and holds two conclusions. First, that power so taken does not signify a relation. Second conclusion: that it signifies something absolute, and that this is the principle of begetting.

{#s032-04} But does this power belong to the Son and the Holy Spirit? It is said not, because in them there is repugnance of terms. But if you ask what the power of begetting in God is, it is said that one need not posit a principle by which someone is able, but the sole non-repugnance of terms suffices. Note also that it is the Doctor’s intention that the persons are constituted by relations. And although the Father is formally Father by paternity, nevertheless paternity is not that by which the Father formally acts, but [something] absolute.

{#s032-05} But is there in the Son a power of begetting? It is said that there is, because there is in him the fruitful memory together with the intellect, which is the principle of begetting. Why, then, does he not beget? It is said that this is because the Father has, or has beforehand, an adequate term through the eternal generation. For in the Father and the Son there is the same essence, the same memory; but because the memory has an adequate term, it cannot extend to others; and so the Son cannot beget another Son. [[p. 54|v° · Di.viii. Q.i]] And this inability is not on the part of the Son but on the part of the term, which term is impossible in God. But can the Father have several Sons? It is said not, for the reason given, because he has an adequate term.

{#s032-06} Note the conclusions you can draw from Scotus’s text. First, that generation in God is univocal. Second, that the individual difference is more perfect than the specific in creatures, not in God. Third, that the difference between the things constituted is not as great as that between the constituents. Fourth, that in generation the aspect of assimilation is more perfect than the aspect of distinction. Fifth, that not every form that gives being gives acting. This is plain: for paternity gives being to the Father, but the Father does not act by paternity. To the argument at the beginning and to Augustine it is said that he understands [it] formally and not fundamentally, as the question asks.
