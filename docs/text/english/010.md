{#s010-h .heading} [Third question.]

{#s010-01} The third question of the first distinction, where it is asked whether enjoyment is an act of the will or a passion, that is, whether enjoyment is that delight which follows the act of enjoying, or that beatific act. It is proved that it is delight: fruit is the last thing that is awaited from trees, and *to enjoy* (*frui*) is said from *fruit* (*fructus*); but the last thing awaited is delight, since it follows upon the act, *Ethics* X; therefore etc. To the contrary: the will, by an elicited act, loves God; therefore either for the sake of something else, and then it uses [him] and so is perverse, or for his own sake, and then it enjoys, by the definition of enjoyment; and thus to enjoy is an act and not a passion.

{#s010-02} In this question three things will be considered. First, whether love and delight are really the same. Second, whether enjoyment is love or delight. Third, whether enjoyment is an act of the will. On the first article there is the opinion of Thomas, that love and delight are really the same but differ in concept. The Doctor refutes both parts. The first: for in the demons there is love, since they love themselves, but they do not take delight. He refutes the second: for love is elicited by the will, but delight is not; it follows upon it. [[p. 21|r° · Di.i. Q.iiii]] And so the Doctor concludes that they are really distinct.

{#s010-03} As to the second, it is said that love is an operation elicited by the will which presupposes cognition of the object, whereas delight is a certain operation received in the will and caused by the object. But it is the Doctor’s opinion that enjoyment implies both love and delight; hence Augustine, *On the Trinity* X: “We enjoy things known in which the will, delighted for their own sake, rests.”

{#s010-04} As to the third, whether enjoyment is an act of the will, there is the opinion of Giles in the present distinction: *to enjoy* either signifies the act by which a separated end, not previously possessed, is attained, and in this sense enjoyment is an act of the intellect, for the beatific object is first attained by the intellect; or *to enjoy* signifies the act by which an end already possessed is attained, and in this sense it is an act of the will. Thus, according to him, enjoyment belongs principally to the act of the intellect, and secondarily to the act of the will, inasmuch as it signifies a certain delight consequent upon the act of the intellect. But the Doctor refutes this and says that enjoyment is an act of the will, as is clear from Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine* I: “To enjoy is to cleave by love”; but love is in the will; therefore etc. And if anyone asks how, then, enjoyment and delight are related, the answer is: as a subject and its proper attribute, *Ethics* X.

{#s010-05} To the argument at the beginning I say that fruit is not so called as something awaited to be possessed, but as something awaited to be tasted; and upon this tasting delight follows as a passion. Thus we shall not enjoy that [delight]; rather, it follows upon our enjoyment.
