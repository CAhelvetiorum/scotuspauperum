{#s011-h .heading} [Fourth question.]

{#s011-01} The fourth question of the first distinction, where it is asked whether, when the ultimate end has been apprehended by the intellect, the will can not-will [it]. It seems not: for the end as such has the character of every good, and the will is naturally inclined to that; therefore it tends to it necessarily. To the contrary: if the will were necessitated, it would neither merit nor demerit; but the truth is the contrary, *Ethics* III.

{#s011-02} The Doctor divides this question into four articles. In each article Henry of Ghent is brought in, from *Quodlibet* III q. 57 and *Quodlibet* IV q. 2, and Thomas, *Prima secundae* q. 10, articles 1 and 2. These say that once the ultimate end has been apprehended, the will enjoys it necessarily. Again, while the Doctor proceeds against Henry [to show] that God by his absolute power can cause the vision of himself without enjoyment, in one paragraph where he says: “It is said that that major premise is true of absolute things neither of which depends on the other,” etc., Henry’s reply is given, *Quodlibet* XII q. 5.

{#s011-03} But coming, as is customary, to the Doctor’s opinion: it is said that this question can be understood [[p. 22|v° · Di.i. Q.iiii]] in four ways. First, of the end apprehended obscurely in general. Second, of the end apprehended obscurely in particular, as we conceive beatitude in the triune God. Third, of the end clearly seen by one who has a will supernaturally elevated, that is, by one who has a will perfected by a supernatural habit, namely that of charity. Fourth, of the end clearly seen by one who has no supernatural habit in the will, supposing that God by his absolute power were to show himself to the intellect without giving any supernatural habit to the will.

{#s011-04} To the first the Doctor says that, when the end is apprehended in general, just as the will does not necessarily enjoy the things that are for the end, neither [does it necessarily enjoy] that end apprehended universally and in common. To the second it is likewise said that it does not necessarily enjoy the end seen in particular and obscurely. To the third: the will elevated by the habit of charity does not necessarily enjoy, as far as it is concerned. To the fourth he says that a will not supernaturally elevated can enjoy the ultimate end presented to it. The holy Doctor holds, in the place cited, that once the will clearly sees the object, that vision and the fittingness of the object necessitate the will. But the Doctor concludes that the will cannot be coerced.

{#s011-05} But the whole force and difficulty in this matter lies in the words of Augustine, *On the Trinity* XIII c. 3, where he says that all men naturally wish to be happy; therefore they necessarily will the ultimate end in which beatitude consists. To this the Doctor replies that Augustine’s saying is to be understood habitually, that is, of natural inclination, and not actually. He says further that there is no enjoyment in habitual appetite, but only in actual appetite. Note here also that, although the will can [go] to willing and to its opposite, which is not-willing, it cannot go to its contrary, which is hating God. For this is against the nature of the will, since hating God is evil without qualification, which is not included under the object of the will, *Ethics* III; for there you have it that the good, absolutely, is the object of the will.

{#s011-06} But suppose someone says that the damned hate God, from that psalm, “those who hate you.” It is answered that *hatred* is taken here, “those who hate you,” as “those who refused.” But [suppose someone asks whether] our will can be necessitated by some cause. It is answered that the will can be considered in four ways: as passive, as receptive, as active and as productive. In the first two ways it is necessitated, since being acted upon is not in the power of the patient; but in the other two ways it is not necessitated.

{#s011-07} To the argument at the beginning, [I answer] by saying that it is understood of natural appetite or inclination as a habit, not of actual appetite.
