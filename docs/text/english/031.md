{#s031-h .heading} [Sixth distinction. Single question.]

{#s031-01} On the sixth distinction it is asked whether God the Father begot God the Son by will. It seems that he did. Hilary, in *On the Trinity* [book] XV, says that God the Father communicates all things to the Son by charity and by nature; but charity is a perfection of the will; therefore [he begot him] by charity and by will. To the contrary: Augustine, in *On the Trinity* III, says that the heresy of Eunomius, who says that the Son of God is [Son] not by nature but by will, is utterly to be cast out.

{#s031-02} At the beginning of the solution [Scotus] sets out the opinion of Henry, in the *Summa*, article 58, question 2, who says that the Father did not beget the Son willingly but by natural necessity. And when, in the third article, he says “another doctor says otherwise, that the intellect etc.”, this is Thomas, *Summa*, first part, question 34, article 1, and Henry of Ghent, *Quodlibet* VI, question 2, [and] in the *Summa*. These say that in the intellect there are two things, namely understanding and saying, and that the Son is in act through understanding and not through saying. In the second article, where it is shown that the Father does not beget the Son by the will as an eliciting principle, [there is the view] that some draw a distinction about the word “by will”, i.e. “voluntarily”; and so they say that “by will” can be taken nominally or adverbially. These [views] may have their origin in Brother John of Rodington, Book I, distinction 6, single question, article 3. And near the end of the question he says: “Yet as to Augustine’s meaning concerning the intention that joins parent with offspring, a certain doctor says etc.”; this is Henry, *Quodlibet* II, question 5.

{#s031-03} But coming to the question, it is said that God the Father begot [the Son] willingly, not in the sense that the will is the principle of the Son, but in this sense: in the first instant of origin the Father understands formally, and then he can also have an act of willing formally; in the second instant of origin he begets the Son. Yet he does not will this begetting by a volition that follows the begetting, but by the volition had in the first instant of origin, by which the Father formally wills, presupposing in some way, formally, the act of understanding by which the Father formally understands, but not the begetting of the Word.

{#s031-04} But from this question the following difficulty arises. If the Father did not beget the Son by will, in the sense that the will is the productive principle, but by nature (according to Augustine in the letter to Orosius, where he says, “the will of the Father cannot go before his knowledge”, and Damascene, “Generation is a work of nature”), it is asked, then, whether the Father begot the Son by necessity. To make this clear, four things will be done: first, it will be said what necessity is; second, [we come] to the point of the question; third, four conclusions will be set out; fourth, it will be said whether the Father begot the Son naturally or not.

{#s031-05} To the first it is said that necessity is an intrinsic degree of all divine things, [[p. 51|r° · Di.vi. Q.i · sig. d.2]] as infinity is an intrinsic degree of some divine things. Considering these three, the essence, the attributes and the relations: these are necessary being. But infinity, though it belongs to the divine essence and to the attributes, does not belong to the relative properties; for otherwise there would be some perfection in the Father that would not be in the Son and the Holy Spirit, which is false. But what is an intrinsic degree? It is said: one that does not vary the formal notion of something, as [the degrees] intense and remiss [do not]. But are there several necessities in God? It is said not.

{#s031-06} As to the second article, whether the Father produced the Son by necessity, it is said that necessity is taken in four ways: first, as it is opposed to contingency; second, the necessity of violence; third, the necessity of exigency; fourth, the necessity of indigence. It is said, then, that the Father begot the Son by necessity in the first and third ways, not in the second and fourth. The third is plain, for every perfect memory requires a word. Of the second, namely violence, and of the fourth, namely [indigence], it is said not, because if he were indigent, or necessitated by another, he would not be perfect. Of the first it is plain that he did, namely by the necessity opposed to contingency, for the Son is not contingent, nor is his generation.

{#s031-07} But how does the Father produce the Word? I say: by the intellect. For this I set out four conclusions. First, that God the Father begets the Son with the intellect preceding. This is proved: the essentials precede the notionals; but the intellect is essential, the production notional; therefore etc. Second conclusion: that he produces with the intellect concomitant, because intellect and generation are coeternal, and consequently the one accompanies the other. Third conclusion: that [he does] not [produce] with the intellect following. This is plain, because a power does not follow its act but precedes it. Fourth conclusion: that the Father produces the Word with the intellect eliciting [it]. This is plain, because according to Augustine a word never exists except from one who speaks; but there is no other power in God that speaks except the intellect; therefore the intellect elicits the Word. And just as there are four conclusions with respect to the [intellect], set out four others with respect to the act of the will. But which perfections of the intellect concur in eliciting the Word? It is said: wisdom, for it has the character [of a principle] with respect to the Word; whereas knowledge (*scientia*) is of creatures, prudence of contingent things, art of things to be made; and so it alone concurs.

{#s031-08} As to the third, there are certain conclusions. First, that some things are necessary being, as all divine things. Second, that some things are impossible being, as a chimera. Third conclusion: that some things are possible being, as those that are in objective potency. Fourth conclusion: that some things are possible not-being, as all things created in act.

{#s031-09} As to the fourth, [[p. 52|v° · Di.vii. Q.i]] before I come to the point of the question, it is said that these four are essentially ordered, namely substance, nature, essence, quiddity. For whatever is a substance is a nature, as Socrates, but not conversely. But I understand it thus: what says “nature” says “substance”, because nature includes two things, and not conversely. Second, if something is a nature it is an essence, not conversely. This is plain, for the things that are in objective potency have, according to some, an essence, and we have knowledge of them; and granting that they have an essence, they nevertheless do not have a nature. Third, [if] it is an essence, therefore [it is] a quiddity, not conversely; for in God there are many quidditative notions, but only one essence.

{#s031-10} But coming to the point, whether the Father begot the Son naturally: it is said that “nature” is taken in four ways: first, as distinguished from the supernatural; second, as distinguished from the violent; third, as distinguished from the free; fourth, as distinguished from the artificial. The answer, then, is that the Father begot the Son naturally in the second, third and fourth ways, but not in the first. But can the Son be said in some way to be begotten by will? To this it is said that there are authorities of Augustine that mean to prove that he can; but they are to be understood [thus]: the will as complacent, not as originating principle, can have an act with respect to the begetting of the Word. This is against Godfrey, *Quodlibet* V, question 4, [who holds] that the Father does not beget the Son willingly in any way.

{#s031-11} Note, roughly in the middle of the question, that the divine intellect has one act corresponding to our act of understanding, which is a quality: the act, namely, by which the Father’s intellect formally understands. It also has an act corresponding to an act in the genus of action, by which it expresses the Word. And some say that this is the fruitful memory, which according to the Scotists is the principle of the Word and through which the Word is produced. And so it can be held that the intellect has two acts, one in the genus of action, the other in the genus of quality. This is described by others in another way: for they say that in God there are two acts, namely operation and production. Operation is an immanent act; but by production something is terminated, namely the Son as produced. Second, you can draw from the text that in the will there is a twofold act: first, to love the object and its knowledge; second, to move the intelligence, and [this] joins parent with offspring. To the argument at the beginning it is said that [Hilary] means [begetting] by the charity that is the essence.
