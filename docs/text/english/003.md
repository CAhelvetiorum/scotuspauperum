{#s003-h .heading} [[p. 6|v°]] Prologue

{#s003-01} On the first question of the Prologue to the *Sentences*: whether it is necessary for man in the present state that some special supernatural doctrine be inspired, namely one which he could not attain by the natural light of the intellect.

{#s003-02} It seems not: every power that has something common as its first natural object can extend to that object and to whatever is contained under it. But our power has being *qua* being as its object; therefore it can extend to being and to whatever is contained under being. But God is contained under being; therefore it can extend to God naturally. The major premise is Aristotle’s, *De anima* II; the minor is Avicenna’s, *Metaphysics* I, c. 5.

{#s003-03} To the contrary, in Baruch 3 it is said of wisdom: “There is none that can know her ways.”

{#s003-04} The first opinion is set out two pages further on, where it is said: “Against this question I argue and prove that whatever necessary truths”, etc. It is drawn from the side of the middle term and of the extremes, and it belongs to the solemn doctor who is called Henry “the Sorbonist” from his college, or “of Ghent” from his homeland, in his *Summa*; in Scotus it begins: “This argument is against the philosophers.”

{#s003-05} The fourth argument against the philosophers, which begins “Fourthly it is argued thus: what is ordered to some end”, etc., is Thomas’s, in the First Part of the *Summa*, q. 1, art. 1, in the principal solution; it is drawn from the end and from the things ordered to the end.

{#s003-06} The fifth argument, which begins “Fifthly I argue thus: no agent”, etc., is Henry of Ghent’s in the *Summa*, and William of Ware’s in Book I, q. 2; it is drawn from the nature of an instrument and of the one who uses it. And in the paragraph “This argument seems to conclude against him”, the Subtle Doctor speaks against him [i.e. against its author] when he says: “because such [truth], according to him, cannot be had”, etc.

{#s003-07} Henry indeed has these things in the *Summa*, art. 1; and yet, once the question has been discussed on the theologians’ side, he replies to the philosophers’ grounds.

{#s003-08} “To the second argument”, he says, “look up Thomas’s reply”: so says Scotus, and Thomas is indicated there, First Part of the *Summa*, question 1, article 1, in the solution of the last argument. And because Thomas’s solution is subtly attacked by the Subtle Doctor, a second solution is added in the paragraph where it is said: “The said reply to the second argument, however”, etc. This is Thomas’s, First Part, q. 54, art. 2, chiefly in the solution of the second argument; and Godfrey’s, *Quodlibet* 14, q. 4, or c. 9.

{#s003-09} “To the third it is replied thus, that the first principles”, etc.: this is the solution of Henry of Ghent in the *Summa*. And when it is solved otherwise a second time, where it is said “Therefore it is said otherwise, that the terms of a first principle”, etc., this is taken ⟨expressly⟩ from the First Part of the *Summa*. And when this second solution has been rejected, he replies a third time at the words “Another reply is given, that from the major premises alone”, etc. This belongs to Brother William [[p. 7|r° · Q.ii]] of Ware, Book I, q. 2, in the solution of the fifth argument.

{#s003-10} At length he comes down to the principal arguments of the question in order to complete his question, and says at the beginning: “To the first I distinguish concerning the natural object.” This solution comes from Brother William of Ware, Book I, q. 2, in the solution of the second argument.

{#s003-11} But coming to the Doctor’s own opinion, he answers the title [of the question] in the affirmative; and this can be had from Augustine, *De Trinitate* XV, last chapter, who says: “What is the cause”, he says, “why you cannot see that very light with a steady gaze, if not infirmity? And who made it so for you, if not iniquity?” The philosophers, however, wished to say that no doctrine supernaturally inspired was necessary; indeed, that anyone can acquire naturally all the knowledge necessary to him. But the theologians, and the Doctor, say the contrary: for otherwise the intellect could not understand [God] except under something common and under the aspect of being, which is false. Therefore one must hold with Scotus that it is necessary for man that some doctrine be supernaturally inspired.

{#s003-12} To make this evident, note that a receptive or intellective power can be compared to the act which it receives; and in this way no supernaturality is found, because it is naturally inclined to any intellection whatever and is, as it were, naturally perfected [by it]. In a second way, however, it can be compared to the agent from which it receives; and then every cognition which cannot be caused by the agent intellect and the phantasm is supernatural to it. Such are many complex truths about God, as “God begets”, “God is three and one”, and the like: for all such truths are had supernaturally and by inspiration.

{#s003-13} To the argument at the beginning it is said that it is true that God can be known under the aspect of being, but not of such a being, namely God.
