{#s008-h .heading} [Book One. First Distinction. First Question.]

{#s008-01} On the first distinction it is asked first whether the *per se* object of enjoyment is the ultimate end. It seems not, by the authority of Augustine, *Eighty-three Questions*, q. 36: “Invisible goods are to be enjoyed.” But there are many invisible goods; therefore it is not only the ultimate end that is to be enjoyed. To the contrary is Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine*, c. 1: “The things to be enjoyed are the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit”; and these three are one thing; therefore, etc.

{#s008-02} In this question the opinion of Avicenna, *Metaphysics* IX, c. 4, is set out, who says that ordered enjoyment can be about something other than the ultimate end; the Doctor refutes it in many ways. And after the arguments of others he brings in the arguments of Bonaventure, Book I, dist. 1, art. 2, q. 3, and argues from the notion of the image, which is capable of God; and he argues against Avicenna. And this is set out where the Doctor says: “Others argue against this opinion.” Secondly, [he brings in] the argument of blessed Thomas, First Part of the *Summa*, q. 12, art. 1, which is taken from the fact that our soul is created immediately by God: he concludes, therefore, that it is beatified immediately in him, and so it does not come to rest except in God alone, against Avicenna. And this is set out there: “Others argue against his reasoning thus: the soul is created.” But for fuller explanation three things will be considered: first, the object of ordered enjoyment; second, the object of enjoyment in general; third, how enjoyment is understood to be about the end.

{#s008-03} As to the first, it is said that ordered enjoyment has the ultimate end for its object. And the reason is that, just as the intellect is to assent to the first truth alone for its own sake, so the will is to assent to the first good alone for its own sake. As to the second, it is said that the object of enjoyment in general is the ultimate end: either the true end, namely that which is the ultimate end by the nature of the thing; or the apparent end, namely that which is shown by erring reason as if it were the ultimate end; or the practical end, which the will of its freedom wills as its ultimate end. As to the third, it is said that enjoyment is about the end as a concomitant object.

{#s008-04} And note in this question, in the second reply, that our appetite, although it is finite subjectively, being in a finite soul, is nonetheless infinite objectively, because it comes to rest only in an infinite object. Secondly, note in the same reply that between God and us there is a vital proportion [[p. 18|v° · Di.i. Q.ii]] of change: for as much as God can change [us], so much can our intellect be changed. Thirdly, note at the end, where he says “To the question I say”, that not just any form satisfies the appetite of matter totally and extensively, so that no [one] form can satisfy all its appetites; but one [form] satisfies one [appetite] most perfectly, namely the most perfect form.

{#s008-05} But which act is worse: to use what is to be enjoyed, or to enjoy what is to be used? On this it is to be noted, according to Ambrose, that there are four kinds of virtues in our soul. Some are for regulating, as prudence; some for restraining an act, as temperance; some for intensifying, as fortitude; some for both, as justice, one part of which is remunerative, and this is for intensifying, and is called by Aristotle distributive justice; the other is punitive, and this is for restraining, and is called by Aristotle (*Ethics* V) commutative justice.

{#s008-06} Now when a virtue is given for intensifying an act, the vice is greater in falling short than in excess, as timidity is blamed more than boldness (*Ethics* III). The reverse holds of the virtue given for restraining an act, as temperance (*Ethics* VII). Now to our purpose: the virtue of friendship, which, if it is not a virtue, is with virtue (*Ethics* VIII), is given for intensifying. And therefore the vice of those who love nothing is greater and more blamed; for he who takes delight in nothing is far from being a man and has not obtained the name of man (*Ethics* III). And these are blamed more than those who are too much given to love. Now those who use what is to be enjoyed, that is, who love God for the sake of something else, fall short, because they do not love as much as they ought; but those who enjoy what is to be used, that is, who love a creature for its own sake, exceed, because they love more than they ought. Hence it is that to use what is to be enjoyed is a greater vice than to enjoy what is to be used.

{#s008-07} But which act is supremely vicious? I say that the supremely meritorious act is the act of love of God, and the act contrary or opposed to this is supremely demeritorious or vicious.

{#s008-08} To the argument at the beginning: [Augustine] will have meant the end which is the essence, under which the persons are contained.
