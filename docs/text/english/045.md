{#s045-h .heading} [Fourth question.]

{#s045-01} The fourth question of the twelfth distinction, where it is asked whether the eternal Father, prior in origin, produces or spirates [[p. 74|v° · Di.xii. Q.iiii]] the Holy Spirit before the Son does. On this four things will be seen. First, whether in God there is an order of origin. Second, whether in God the Father is prior in origin to the Son. Third, whether in that prior in which the Father precedes the Son some operation belongs to the Father; or whether the Father spirates the Holy Spirit prior in origin to the Son. Fourth, whether the Father produces the Son prior in origin to the Holy Spirit. And although some things about these were said in the question above, they will be treated more fully here.

{#s045-02} As to the first, it is said that in God there is an order of origin. For wherever there are some distinct things, unless they are ordered they are necessarily confused; but the persons are distinct; therefore they are ordered, since they are not confused. But order implies prior and posterior; and so in God there will be prior and posterior. But here the holy doctor says, First Part, question 41, article 3, that there is indeed order in God, but not prior and posterior. Against this: Augustine, *City of God* 10, says that prior and posterior have the same relation to order as even and odd to number; but there cannot be even or odd without number, and conversely, since they are as subject and proper attribute; therefore neither is there order without prior and posterior, and conversely.

{#s045-03} Others say that there is indeed a first, a middle and a last, but not prior and posterior. Against this in four ways. First, grammatically: for in grammar one declines *pridem, prior, primum*; and so *first* presupposes the comparative and the positive, and consequently presupposes *prior*. Second, it is argued logically: for the logician posits ten first genera; but the other, lower genera are genera, but not first; therefore they are prior or posterior. Third, physically: for the natural philosopher teaches and demonstrates that there is a first mover; it is clear that, according to him, many things are prior; therefore there is prior and posterior. Fourth, theologically: blessed Dionysius, *On the Angelic Hierarchy*, says that in any order one proceeds from the posterior to the prior, and up to one hierarch, and finally there is one seraph, and consequently one first. By these four reasons one must grant that in God there is prior and posterior, and consequently order. This is confirmed by Augustine, *On the Threefold Dwelling*, who says that in the homeland we shall see that the Father is prior in origin to the Son. Some say that no such authority is found; saving their grace, either they have not seen it or they have not drawn it rightly from that text.

{#s045-04} As to the second article, whether the Father is prior in origin to the Son, the Doctor says yes. And it is argued in this way: wherever this is from that, that from which this is, is prior in origin, and that which is from it is posterior. But in God the Father and the Son are so [[p. 75|r° · Di.xii. Q.iiii]] related that the Son is from the Father; therefore the Father is prior, since he is that from which, and the Son posterior in origin, since he is from another. This truth is confirmed by blessed Augustine, who says in *On the Trinity* 1 that there is no thing that begets itself. And here no reason can of necessity be assigned except that the same thing would be prior to itself; for if that were not unfitting, it would be possible that something existing prior to itself should exist posterior to itself. But since Augustine does not give that cause explicitly, he adduces Anselm, *Monologion*, first book. The intellect, says Anselm, does not grasp that the same thing should give itself being prior to itself, since the same thing would be prior to itself. But the Father gives being to the Son; it is not unfitting that something be prior to another; therefore the Father is distinguished from the Son to whom he gives being.

{#s045-05} As to the third, whether the Father spirates the Holy Spirit prior in origin to the Son, I say yes: for whatever the Father has, he has from himself; but he has spiration; therefore from himself; and consequently he spirates from himself, and the Son from another. And that is to spirate prior in origin, that is, to spirate from oneself and not from another. But on this see better in the preceding question.

{#s045-06} As to the fourth, whether the Father produces the Son prior in origin to the Holy Spirit, the answer to this is yes: for just as the Father is prior to the Son, so he also spirates prior to the Son. But this is not a prior such that it can be called a prior *in which*; it is only a prior *from which*. Hence in no prior that can be assigned does the Father spirate first. But is there an order of nature in God? Some say that it is not Scotus’s intention to posit an order of nature in God; others say that it is. But a certain one says that an order of nature can be understood in two ways. In one way, for the order of nature that holds between nature and nature; and such an order is in no way to be posited in God, since there is only one nature there. In another way it can be understood for the order that holds between natural perfections; and such an order of nature is there, just as an essential order is posited there, not between essence and essence, since there is only one, but between essential perfections. And since among these perfections there is no order of origin, because none of them is from itself (which an order of origin requires), and since there is no confusion among them, it follows that, if an order is posited, it is an order of nature in the manner just stated; understanding the order, moreover, with respect to natural understanding, so that one is apt to be understood first and the other later, but not so that such an order is posited in the thing absolutely, for that would be false, since one essential perfection is not in itself naturally prior to another.
