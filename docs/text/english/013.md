{#s013-h .heading} [Second distinction. First question.]

{#s013-01} On the second distinction, in which several questions are asked. He first puts the first [question], in which two are posed. One concerns reality, namely whether there is among beings something actually infinite. The second is whether *God exists* is known through itself. It seems not [that there is something actually infinite]. For Aristotle says in *Physics* VIII that if there were [[p. 24|v° · Di.ii. Q.i]] an infinite power it would move in no time; but nothing moves in no time; therefore no nature is infinite. To the contrary: in *Physics* VIII it is proved that the first mover is of infinite power, because it moves with an infinite motion.

{#s013-02} To the first argument Brother William of Ware replies, in part falsely, in d. 2 of this first [book], q. 1, in solving the fifth argument. The Doctor indicates this when he says: “It is replied that the major is true,” etc. It should be noted, however, that some books have here a third question, which begins “Whether there is some being that is simply first”; but it is not part of the text. He solves the second question first, because the logic of the proposition known through itself is most subtly discussed. He thus infers negative corollaries. First, that there is no [valid] distinction between [what is] known through itself in itself and known to nature and to us, as Thomas distinguished in the first part of the *Summa*, q. 2, a. 1, in the principal solution. Second, that there is no [valid] distinction of this kind, namely that one thing is known through itself to us wise men and another to the unwise, as Thomas likewise did in the same place, [and] in the *Prima secundae*, q. 94, a. 2. Third, that the distinction is invalid by which some things are known through themselves in the first order and some in the second, as William of Ware distinguished in book I, d. 2, q. 8.

{#s013-03} Having treated the second question, at the beginning of the solution of the first he says: the exemplar cause is not a different genus of cause from the efficient cause, as Henry held in *Quodlibet* IX, qq. 1 and 2. In the proof of the first question, in the second principal article, he proves that [God’s] understanding and willing of himself are the same as the essence of the first. In the first [proof] the argument from the identity of power and object is introduced, and it belongs to Thomas, *On the Sentences* I, d. 35, q. 1, in solving the third argument; Giles of Rome has the same in *Quodlibet* III. Second, he then proves that the understanding of all things is in the first [being] identical with the first. In the fifth proof he says: “Again, fifthly, one could argue thus: this intellect is nothing but a certain act of understanding.” From the identity of intellect and understanding it is there concluded that, if there is one intellect of all things, its understanding is also unique; and so the proposition [is proved]. So argues Alexander of Alessandria, of the Order of Friars Minor, [holding] the office of general, d. 35, q. 2, in solving the second argument.

{#s013-04} Further, the Doctor proves the infinity [of God] by four ways, etc. But the infinity of the first is [also] proved through its essence in this manner: because it creates, and between the extremes of creation there is an infinite distance. This is the argument of Henry, *Quodlibet* IV, last question, and of Richard of Middleton on the second distinction of [Book] I, a. 4, q. 4. Finally, after the four ways, the proposition is proved from the negation of matter, because form is limited by matter. This is the argument of Thomas, first part of the *Summa*, q. 7, a. 1.

{#s013-05} But coming to the opinion [[p. 25|r° · Di.i. Q.i]] of Scotus, it is said on the second question: some say that *God exists* is not known through itself. Their reason is that what is demonstrable is not known through itself; but *God exists* is demonstrable, by the Philosopher, *Physics* VIII, and by Avicenna, *Metaphysics* I c. 1; therefore *God exists* is not known through itself. But it is said that *God exists* and *the divine essence exists* are propositions known through themselves, since they have evident truth from their terms. They are the immediate [propositions] into which are resolved all propositions stating anything about God, however conceived. This is Scotus’s understanding of propositions known through themselves: their extremes are naturally apt to produce evidence of that combination for anyone who perfectly apprehends the extremes of that combination.

{#s013-06} To the first question it is said that God is infinite. But his infinity cannot be demonstrated a priori, only a posteriori. Before I come to declaring this truth, I shall make some preliminary propositions. The first: there is something first which cannot be produced; otherwise there would be a process to infinity. The second proposition: that first is uncausable; this is clear as with the first, otherwise there would be a process to infinity. The third proposition: that first productive [being] is actually existent of itself. This is clear: if it were in potency and were reduced to act, it would have to be reduced by something else, and so something else productive would be prior to it. Then among these propositions he sets this: that unity of the first nature must be granted. The fourth proposition: its understanding and willing are nothing other than its essence, since it is the same [thing that is] principle and end. The fifth proposition: to understand itself is the same as that nature, because nothing is loved unless known; therefore if loving itself is necessary being of itself, it follows that understanding itself is too. The sixth proposition: no act of understanding can be an accident of the first nature, since it understands through its essence and does not beg from things outside.

{#s013-07} On these suppositions he shows infinity in God by four ways. First, by the way of efficiency, a way the Philosopher proves in *Physics* VIII and *Metaphysics* XII. He argues thus: the first moves with an infinite motion; therefore it has infinite power. The second way, through understanding, thus: the first understands distinctly all knowables, and so on; but these are infinite, and it understands them by a single act; therefore it is of infinite power. The third way is by way of the end, thus: our will does not rest in anything finite, but our will rests only in the highest good; therefore that is infinite. The fourth way from eminence, thus: it is impossible that anything be more perfect than the most eminent; but it is not impossible that something be more perfect than the finite; therefore the first being is infinite.

{#s013-08} But note that Scotus seems to contradict himself on propositions known through themselves. For in the first [conclusion], where he answers the question, he says that *God exists* is known through itself; then the second conclusion is given, where he says, “And if it is asked,” etc., from which one has it that *God exists* is not known through itself. Some [[p. 26|v° · Di.ii. Q.ii]] resolve this contradiction thus: in the first conclusion he is speaking of intuitive knowledge, in the second of abstractive. But the son of a certain [master] resolves it thus: existence can belong to God in the first mode of saying *per se* or in the second. If it belongs in the first, it is known through itself, and so the first conclusion holds. If in the second mode of saying *per se*, it is not known through itself, because it can be proved a priori. And in this way the second conclusion is understood, and they do not contradict each other.

{#s013-09} To the argument at the beginning, [we answer] by saying that the Philosopher in *Physics* VIII meant that if there is an infinite power and it acts by necessity of nature, it acts in no time; but God does not act by necessity of nature but by will.
