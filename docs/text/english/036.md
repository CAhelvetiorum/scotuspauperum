{#s036-h .heading} [Fourth question.]

{#s036-01} The fourth question of the eighth distinction, where it is asked whether, together with the highest unity, there can stand a distinction of the essential perfections that in some way precedes the act of the intellect. It seems not: for where there is multitude, there is no simplicity; but in God there is multitude; therefore no simplicity. To the contrary is Dionysius, who says that the manifold formation of the divine perfections is united; and where [it is] united, there [it is] simple.

{#s036-02} In this question the opinion of Thomas is set out first, on the first [book of the] *Scriptum*, distinction 2, and of Hervaeus, [who] say that the divine attributes are only so called [[p. 59|r° · Di.viii. Q.iiii]] through the act of our intellect. And afterwards, when he says, “Others explain this position thus”, Godfrey is touched on, *Quodlibet* VII, question 1. And when he puts forward, “There is another position, which says etc.”, this is Henry, *Quodlibet* V, question 1, and *Quodlibet* XIII, question 1: that the divine attributes are distinguished through the act of the divine intellect. Next to the beginning of the arguments against him the Doctor says: “It is said to this that the attributes are not foundations etc.”; this is Henry, *Quodlibet* V, question 3, and in the *Summa*, article 37, question 7.

{#s036-03} But in this question I shall do three things. First, I shall say what unity is. Second, some conclusions will be set down. Third, whether the attributes are distinguished through the intellect, which is the point at issue.

{#s036-04} To the first I say that unity is formally indivision. And understand that the words “unity” and “indivision” are both privative. As to the second, before the conclusions are set down, I say that unity is taken in four ways. The first degree is [the unity] of aggregation, like a heap of stones. The second, of order or ordering, as in a war [ordered] to its leader: for a war is called one from the unity of the leader. The third, of composition, as a composite. The fourth degree is of the unity of indivision, because it can in no way be divided, or has no identity with another.

{#s036-05} With these points noted, conclusions are set down. The first conclusion: that the attributes in their formal *rationes* have no unity [with one another]; this is plain because they are supremely simple, as intellect and will. The second conclusion: that the attributes have true unity in the divine infinity; this is plain because they share in the same divine infinity. The third conclusion: that the attributes have one unity in the reality of God; this is plain, for intellect and will have the same reality in God. The fourth conclusion: that the attributes have true unity with the divine essence; this is plain, because there is one essence in them. The fifth conclusion, which is to the point: that with the highest unity of the essence there is not identity and distinction with respect to the attributes; and this holds while the highest unity stands, as is plain; for they would be supremely one and not supremely one, which is impossible.

{#s036-06} As to the third, which answers the question, it is said that the attributes have non-identity apart from the intellect, and are not formally the same. This is plain: for with no act of the intellect considering [them], the intellect is the productive principle of the word, and the will of volition. Again, because the will is the principle of spirated love, but the intellect is not. But all these hold apart from the act of the intellect; therefore the distinctions are apart from the intellect. [[p. 60|v° · Di.viii. Q.v]] And so we answer the question in the affirmative; and as is said of these, so of the others. To the argument at the beginning it is said that simplicity of essence stands with a multitude of perfections.
