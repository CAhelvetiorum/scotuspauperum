{#s016-h .heading} [Distinction 3. First question.]

{#s016-01} On the third distinction there are eleven questions, and in the first there are two. First, whether God can be known naturally by the intellect of the wayfarer. Second, whether God is what is first known by us naturally in this present state. It seems not, because John 1: “No one has ever seen God”; and every intellectual nature is called vision, according to Augustine, *On the Trinity*. To the contrary: Christ, speaking of knowledge of the Father, says “and you have seen him,” John 4.

{#s016-02} In solving the first, he says that it should not be understood [by the distinction] that God can be known negatively but not affirmatively, as Thomas distinguishes in the First Part of the *Summa*, q. 12, a. 12. Nor, second, should one distinguish between knowledge *that* [he] is and *whether* [he] is. Nor should one distinguish [[p. 30|v° · Di.iii. Q.i]] *whether he is* as it is a question about the truth of a proposition, as Thomas did in the First Part of the *Summa*, q. 3, a. 4, in answering a certain argument. Having set aside these three, and coming down directly to the question, the opinion of the Solemn Doctor in the *Summa*, a. 22, qq. 2 and 3, is set out. After that, the Doctor, following his own mind, posits the univocity of being, granting in brief that it is univocal to God and creature, to substance and accident. And having touched, immediately before the fourth statement, on the opinion about the quiddity of the material thing (which quiddity, according to Thomas, First Part of the *Summa*, q. 12, a. 4, is the adequate object of the wayfarer’s intellect), he disposes of the first [question]. Coming to the second question, having set down a twofold distinction, he lays out the order of the things that are known actually and confusedly.

{#s016-03} But coming to the question, five conclusions are laid down. First, that we can have some concept in which God is conceived *per se* and quidditatively. This is clear: no one conceives a property of a subject as belonging to that subject unless he has a quidditative concept of the subject. But we conceive the attributive properties of God; therefore we have some *per se* quidditative concept of God. Second conclusion: God can be known not only in an analogous concept but in some univocal concept. This is clear, for he is known in the concept of being, which is univocal to God and creature. Third conclusion: God is not known under a specific and proper concept. This is clear: as long as we live in this world, on account of the punishment of sin, we shall be ignorant of the differences of things; therefore *a fortiori* of God’s proper difference. Fourth conclusion: we can arrive at many quidditative concepts of God, namely that he is being, substance, spirit. But these are not proper but common. Fifth conclusion: what we know of God we know through the species of creatures. This is clear from Romans 1: “The invisible things of God [are clearly seen], being understood by the things that are made,” etc.

{#s016-04} On the second question some things must be noted. First, that there is a threefold primacy among knowables: of origin, of perfection and of adequacy. The first two primacies are clear from the Philosopher, *Metaphysics* IX, where it is said that what is prior in generation is posterior in perfection, and conversely. The third primacy is clear from the Philosopher, *Posterior Analytics* I: a property belongs first to its adequate subject, as risible belongs first to man rather than to Peter. Second, that a more perfect intelligible can be so called either simply or proportionally. So we say that the eagle’s seeing of [[p. 31|r° · Di.iii. Q.ii · sig. c.i]] the sun is more perfect simply than my seeing of a candle; but my seeing is more perfect proportionally, for I have more of vision with respect to the candle than the eagle has with respect to the sun. Third, note that knowledge is threefold: actual, habitual and virtual. It is called actual when the object present is actually conceived; habitual when the object is present to the intellect so that the intellect can at once have an elicited act about it; virtual when something intrinsically contained in another is understood in understanding what contains it, as in conceiving man I conceive animal. Some say that this is essential containment; but containment is one thing and knowledge another.

{#s016-05} Then I lay down some conclusions. First, that what is first known by primacy of origin, in confused knowledge, is the most specific species. This is clear from Avicenna, *Metaphysics* I c. 3: metaphysics is last in the order of confused conceiving, or being [is]. Second conclusion: what is first known by distinct knowledge is being. This is clear, for distinct knowledge is through the quiddity, and being is the first quiddity; therefore etc. Again, because if some things are known distinctly, one must go from knowledge to knowledge and begin from being. Third conclusion: in habitual or virtual knowledge, the more common things are known first. The reason is that on the way of generation the more imperfect is prior, because it proceeds from potency to act. Fourth conclusion: what is first known by simple perfection is God. This is clear, for the Philosopher placed happiness in the contemplation of that substance, or of the separate substances (which is truer), *Ethics* X. Fifth conclusion: what is first known by primacy of proportion is the sensible quiddity, for the intellect reaches it more according to the degree of its knowability.

{#s016-06} To the argument at the beginning it is said that it speaks of intuitive knowledge.
