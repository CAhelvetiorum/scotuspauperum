{#s029-h .heading} [Fifth distinction. First question.]

{#s029-01} On the fifth distinction it is asked whether the divine essence begets or is begotten. It seems that it does, because Richard, deliberately opposing the Master, says in *On the Trinity* VIII that it begets. To the contrary: *Extra*, *De summa Trinitate et fide catholica*, where the Master’s view is expressly confirmed.

{#s029-02} In the second article he says, “For this opinion of the Master, so solemnly approved, the following reason is adduced: the begetting thing, etc.” — and this is Adam, book I, d. 33, q. 4, “Whether the divine essence begets.” Below, where he says, “To the other point, ‘the essence is the Father of the Son’: a certain doctor repeats the opinions of others,” this doctor is Henry, in the *Summa*.

{#s029-03} On this question the opinion of Joachim, which is false, is set out; second, the opinion of the Master, which is true. As to the first, it should be known that in this question Abbot Joachim erred, as is found in *Extra*, *De summa Trinitate*, chapter *Damnamus*. His error consists in two things. The first error is that he imputed heresy to Master Peter Lombard, charging him with having posited a quaternity in God, namely three persons and an essence that neither begets nor is begotten; therefore, according to Joachim’s opinion, the essence begets and is begotten. The second error is that he held the unity of the three persons to be not real and true but collective and by likeness. He proves this by the Saviour’s words in John: “Father, I will that they may be one in us, as we also are one.” But Christ’s faithful are not one thing; they are called one because of the unity of faith and charity; therefore the three persons are not one thing. This second error of Joachim was altogether intolerable and heretical, because, as the Pope argues in the decretal cited, the Father in begetting the Son gave the essence [[p. 47|r° · Di.v. Q.i · sig. d.i]] to the Son, and the Father and the Son in producing the Holy Spirit gave the essence to the Holy Spirit. But this communication is not of a part of the essence, because the essence is simple and indivisible. Therefore the whole same essence that is in the Father is in the Son and the Holy Spirit, just as each person is that thing really.

{#s029-04} But coming to the opinion of the Master and of Scotus, and finally of the faithful, it is said that the essence neither begets nor is begotten. This can be proved by Augustine in the letter to Volusianus, where he says: “I would not believe the Gospel if I did not believe the Church.” But the Church has ordained that the essence neither begets nor is begotten (*Extra*, *De summa Trinitate*, in the chapter cited). Therefore in this question three things are to be seen: first, whether the divine essence begets; second, whether it is begotten; third, how the divine essence concurs in generation.

{#s029-05} To the first, the Doctor says that the essence does not beget. He makes this logical argument: whenever some subject is ultimately abstract, nothing can be predicated of it by way of inherence or of something inhering. But the essence is of this kind; therefore nothing can be predicated of it by way of something inhering. This is explained as follows. Adjectives or common accidents can inhere only in a subject apt by nature; and so, if they inhere, they inhere not in abstracts but in concretes. But ‘to beget’ is such an adjective; therefore it belongs to the concrete and the individual (*Metaphysics* I: actions belong to supposits). Therefore it does not belong to the essence, and so the essence will not beget.

{#s029-06} As to the second, by the very same reasons by which it was proved that it does not beget, it will be proved that it is not begotten. From this some statements are laid down. First, the divine essence neither begets nor is begotten, nor spirates nor is spirated; and this by the reasons first stated. Second, the divine essence is neither begetting nor begotten, nor spirating nor spirated: for just as the verb, namely ‘to beget’, does not belong to it, neither does the participle derived from that verb. Third, the divine essence is neither begetting nor conserving, because it is ultimately abstract; therefore these predicates do not belong to it. And these propositions are catholic and theological.

{#s029-07} As to the third, it is said with Augustine and with the ancient doctors, who in divine matters did not dare to name matter, that the essence is neither matter nor quasi-matter, but is communicated as a quiddity to a supposit. How, then, will the essence stand? Some say that the divine essence is a principle *by which* the Son is begotten in such a way that it can in no way be a principle *which*, neither actively nor passively.

{#s029-08} Note, before the middle of the question, that in substance there is only one abstraction, namely of the quiddity from the supposit. In accidents [[p. 48|v° · Di.v. Q.ii]] that are absolute there are two abstractions: one of the accident from the subject, the other of the quiddity from the supposit, for instance of whiteness from this whiteness. In relative accidents there are three abstractions: one of the relation and foundation from the subject; another of the relation from the foundation; a third of the quiddity from the supposit. From this it is clear that the ultimate abstraction is that in which nothing is conceived of the thing except what belongs to its quiddity. Note where he says “to these arguments,” and you will find how the inferior is accidental to the superior; and a little below, that it is not universally true that whatever subject and attribute are predicated of, each is also predicated of the other, nor conversely. And so one can hold that ‘man’ and ‘risible’ are not convertible in every respect: for not everything said of the risible is said of man, since aptitude is said of the risible and is not said of man. To Richard it is said that his authority is not upheld here.
