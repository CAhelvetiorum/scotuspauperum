{#s005-01} The third question of the Prologue. It raises a threefold question at once concerning the object of theology, which regards its material cause. It seems not [that God is the subject of theology], by Boethius, *De Trinitate*: “A simple form”, he says, “cannot be a subject”; therefore neither is God the subject in theology, since he is simple. To the contrary, [Augustine, *The City of God*] VIII, c. 1: “Theology is discourse or reasoning about God.”

{#s005-02} [[p. 10|v°]] When he comes down to the second conclusion, under what aspect God is the object of theology, having laid down the clause on how something can be known in four ways, he infers from it: “Thus another science could be posited concerning God under an aspect of his towards what is outside him, as some posit, namely under the aspect of Restorer.” This is Giles of Rome, *Quodlibet* III, q. 2. “Or [a science] could be posited under some attributal aspect”, etc.: this is Brother William of Ware, Book I, in the Prologue, qq. 1 and 6. Afterwards he says, “Or it could be posited concerning God under a common aspect”: this is Henry in the *Summa*. And this in the first two questions, concerning the theology of necessary truths.

{#s005-03} Then he comes down to the theology of contingent truths, and says in a certain corollary: “From what has been said there is evident the refutation of that opinion which posits Christ as the first subject”, etc. This was the opinion of the holy doctor Bonaventure, as appears in the Prologue to his First Book, q. 1.

{#s005-04} From here the Doctor proceeds to solve the arguments of the first question. In solving the arguments he says below: “As for what is added about the properties”, etc.: this is Brother William of Ware, Book I, dist. 2, q. 3, articles 1 and 2. Then, having finished the first two questions, he begins the third thus: “To the third question it might probably be said thus”; and Henry of Ghent, *Quodlibet* III, q. 6, is introduced.

{#s005-05} ¶ But setting the opinions aside, the Doctor answers the question by saying that theology is about God as its subject; which is proved by Augustine, *The City of God* VIII, who says: “Theology is discourse about God.” The second question is posed: whether theology is about God under some special aspect. The third question: whether all things are referred to that subject.

{#s005-06} “But before I come to the question, I say some things. First, that theology is taken in two ways: for there is theology in itself, and theology in us. I call ‘theology in itself’ that knowledge which the theological object is apt to produce in an intellect proportioned to it, as the divine essence in the divine intellect; but ‘theology in us’ is such knowledge of the thing as our intellect is apt to have of that object. Secondly, I say that theology in itself is divided into the theology of necessary truths, such as are the truths *ad intra*, as ‘God begets’, and the theology of contingent truths, such as are the truths *ad extra*, as ‘God creates the world’. Thirdly, I say that the primary subject of any habit contains virtually all the truths of that habit, whether mediate or immediate.”

{#s005-07} Then to the first question it is said that God is the subject. This is clear: for the most honourable science is about the most honourable being (*Metaphysics* VI); but the most honourable science is theology, and God the noblest being; therefore, etc.

{#s005-08} As to the second question, it is said that something can be known in four ways, as a man can be known: first, that he is a rational animal, and this is proper knowledge; secondly, that he is a substance, and this is common knowledge; thirdly, that he is a tame [[p. 11|r° · Q.iiii]] animal, and this is [knowledge] *per accidens*; fourthly, that he is the noblest animal, and this knowledge is relative. In these four ways God can be known; and so different authors posit the subject in different ways. Henry posits God as the subject under a common aspect; Ware says that he is the subject under the aspect by which he is good; Giles, that he is the subject inasmuch as he is Glorifier and Saviour. But Henry’s position is [knowledge by what is] common, Ware’s [by what is] accidental, Giles’s [by what is] relative: none of them is proper, and therefore they are rejected.

{#s005-09} But for the Doctor’s opinion three conclusions are laid down. The first, that the subject of the theology of necessary truths in itself is God under the aspect of deity. This is clear, for under this [aspect] it contains all the truths of that habit. The second conclusion, that the subject of the theology of contingent truths in itself is the divine essence. This is clear, for contingent truths have their ultimate attribution to it. The third conclusion, that the subject of theology in us is infinite being. This is clear, because that is what is first known to us, and through it we come to the knowledge of God.

{#s005-10} As to the third question, it is said that all things treated in theology are referred to God; and God contains all truths, not only the necessary ones *ad intra*, such as that God begets, that he is three and one, but also the contingent ones *ad extra*, such as that Christ was incarnate of the Virgin, or that God creates the world.

{#s005-11} And note, near the end of these three questions, where it is said that all knowledge which is in the divine intellect is theological; and the reason is that it is had through the theological object, whether it concerns the divine essence or creatures. From which it follows that in his intellect there is no grammar or logic. But it is otherwise in the intellect of the blessed: for beyond the theological truth which he has concerning a creature in the divine essence, he can have natural knowledge of the same things from their own motion [upon his intellect]; and so the blessed can have a science of the creature other than theology.

{#s005-12} To the argument it is said that Boethius is speaking of the subject of an accident, not of the subject of consideration.
