{#s048-h .heading} [Second question.]

{#s048-01} The second question, where it is asked whether any divine person sends itself. For the clarification of this question two things are to be noted. First, that mission is twofold, namely *ad intra* and *ad extra*. Here, however, the question is not about mission *ad intra*, because, as is clear from the Master, who proves from [[p. 79|r° · D.i.xv. Q.iii · sig. f.i]] Augustine that no person is sent except one that has another from whom it is, in this way the Son and the Holy Spirit are sent, but the Father is not. The question here, then, is about mission *ad extra*.

{#s048-02} Second, it is to be noted that in God some things are purely essential, some purely notional, and some mixed of essential and notional. Purely essential are those that belong to the three persons, such as justice, wisdom, ⟨honour⟩ and the like. Purely notional are those that do not belong to the three persons but to one only, such as paternity, filiation and the like. Mixed are said to be those that include something essential and something notional, as when one says *begotten wisdom*: when I say *wisdom* I say something essential; when I say *begotten* I say something notional; so, taking *begotten wisdom* together, I say something essential and notional. Applying this to the matter at hand, I say that this verb *to be sent* is mixed of essential and notional. For in one way it expresses a relation of origin, which belongs to one person only, and everything of that kind is notional. In the second way it expresses a relation to the creature, which belongs to the three persons, and everything of that kind is essential; and this is the efficient causing of creatures, which belongs to the three persons, because the works of the Trinity *ad extra* are undivided, according to Augustine. And what belongs to the three persons in this way can, broadly, be called essential.

{#s048-03} As to the second point, which is the answer to the question, there are two opinions. One is the Master's, who says that a person sends itself, and he proves it thus. According to Augustine, the works of the Trinity *ad extra* are undivided; therefore one person is not sent without another. Again, Ambrose, in book 2 of *On the Holy Spirit*, treating that text of Isaiah, “The Spirit of the Lord is upon me,” etc., says this: “Since it was not defined by the prophet by whom the Son was given, it is shown that he was given by the grace of the Trinity, so that the Son himself also gave himself.”

{#s048-04} The other opinion says the opposite, and proves it thus. To be sent is to be originated; therefore to send is to originate. But no person originates itself; therefore none sends itself. But, holding the Master's opinion, the consequence is denied, because there need not be a correspondence in every respect between the active and the passive, as is clear in *to incarnate* and *to be incarnate*. And so with the Master we answer the question in the affirmative.
