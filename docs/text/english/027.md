{#s027-h .heading} [Fourth distinction. First question.]

{#s027-01} On the fourth distinction it is asked whether this is true: “God begot another God.” It seems that it is: for this is true, “God begot God”; therefore either himself as God or another God. Not himself: *On the Trinity* I, there is no thing whatever that begets itself so as to exist. Therefore another God. To the contrary: there is no other God, Deuteronomy 6: “Hear, O Israel: the Lord thy God is one.”

{#s027-02} In this question there are no opinions; therefore it is said to the question that there is only one God, and so he did not beget another God (Deuteronomy 6: “Hear, O Israel, the Lord thy God is one”). And so I state two conclusions. The first: this is true, “God begot God.” The second: this is false, “God begot another God.” The reason is that to every entity there corresponds adequately some being, or something that is. But the divine essence is a singular entity, in no way capable of plurality, as is clear from what was said above. Therefore there likewise corresponds to it determinately some being, or something that is; though in a thing which is of itself *this*, no other non-identity falls, as such. Therefore, since there one cannot speak of another entity or another deity, neither can one speak there of another God by way of a concrete term corresponding adequately to the deity. Hence this is false: “Socrates is other than Plato in humanity,” because he shares in humanity; but this is true: “Socrates is [[p. 45|r° · Di.iiii. Q.ii]] other than Brownie in humanity,” because Brownie does not share in humanity. Likewise this is false: “The Father is other than the Son in deity.”

{#s027-03} It is to be understood, however, that in this expression ‘other’ conveys two things: the distinction of the things compared with each other, and the community of that in which they are compared, together with its distinction and numbering in them. Hence when it is said, “Socrates is other than Plato in humanity,” what is conveyed is the distinction between Socrates and Plato, the agreement of both in humanity, and the distinction and numbering of humanity in them. Since, then, the deity is not found numbered in the supposits, this is false: “The Father is other than the Son in deity.”

{#s027-04} The first conclusion is clear thus. A personal act belongs to that which is used to signify a supposit; but ‘God’ in the concrete signifies a supposit; therefore a personal act belongs to God. But to beget is a personal act; therefore to beget belongs to God; and so this is true, “God begets God.” But note, for a certain difficult argument (“either he begot another God, or another not-God”): he says that one must grant that he begot ‘not another God’, that is, the person of the Son, who is not other than the deity. But this is denied: “He begot another not-God,” for it would follow that the Son is not God, which is false. To the argument at the beginning I say that he begot the same God, yet not himself. There is a fallacy of figure of speech, by changing a *quale quid* into a *hoc aliquid*: for when I say “he begot the same God” there is no reflexivity, which there is, however, when one says “himself as God.”
