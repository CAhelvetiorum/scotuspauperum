{#s047-h .heading} [Fourteenth and fifteenth distinctions.]

{#s047-01} On the fourteenth and fifteenth distinctions it is asked first whether any divine person whatever sends any person whatever, or is sent. It seems so: for according to Augustine, *To Dardanus*, each person gives and is given; therefore by parity of reason each sends and is sent. To the contrary is Augustine, *On the Trinity* 4, chapter 17: he says the Father is not said to be sent, etc.

{#s047-02} [[p. 78|v° · Di.xiiii.et.xv. Q.ii]] In these distinctions there are no opinions. But coming to the first question, he says no, and adduces the authority of Augustine, *On the Trinity* 4, chapter 7: “The Father is not said to be sent, for he has none from whom he is, or from whom he proceeds.” Therefore *to be sent*, besides expressing a relation *ad extra*, must connote a procession *ad intra*. And then it seems reasonable to say the like of *to send*: for although it principally expresses a relation of action *ad extra*, it nevertheless connotes a relation of active production *ad intra*. And so neither is truly said essentially; rather, *to be sent* belongs only to a produced person, *to send* only to a producing person. And thus there are two sending persons, namely the Father and the Son, and two sent, namely the Son and the Holy Spirit; nor does any person send itself, nor does the produced send the producer.

{#s047-03} This can be had as follows. The Father is said to create through the Word; and in the same way it can be granted that the Son creates through the Holy Spirit, but not conversely. For *to create through the Son* does not express an action *ad extra* absolutely, but an action with authority; and in this it connotes, in the one who works, an active production with respect to that person through whom it works, as it were, sub-authoritatively. So it can be said that to send a person is to work through it, and thus to work an effect with that person by way of the authority of one who gives that person the action, which belongs only to the producer with respect to the produced person. And *to be sent* expresses the same thing: as it were, to work sub-authoritatively by the power of the sending person, which is the case only when the working person receives from another the power of acting.

{#s047-04} And if it is said that the Master says that *to send* is common to the three, because in Isaiah 6 we have: “The Spirit of the Lord is upon me,” and Ambrose: “The Spirit of the Lord has sent me to preach,” the answer is that this is understood of Christ as man, and not of the mission of the eternal Word to be incarnate. But why is *procession* appropriated to the Holy Spirit? It is said that going forth from the will by way of love is procession; but the Holy Spirit goes forth from the will of the Father and the Son by way of love; therefore procession is attributed to him. Secondly, it is so that we may not seem to suffer a poverty of names: since the production of the Son is called generation, the production of the Holy Spirit is called procession to distinguish it from that. To the argument at the beginning it is said that *to be given* and *to be sent* are taken there for *to be communicated*.
