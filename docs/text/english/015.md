{#s015-h .heading} [Third question.]

{#s015-01} The third question of the second distinction, where four questions are asked together. First, whether there are three persons together with the unity of the divine essence. Second, whether there are three persons in the divine essence. Third, whether there can be any intrinsic production in God. Fourth, whether there are only two intrinsic productions there. It seems not, Deuteronomy 32: “See that I alone am.” To the contrary, the Apostle to the Corinthians: “The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ and the communication of the Holy Spirit.”

{#s015-02} When he objects against himself (and he solves the arguments below), he says: “as some say concerning hidden knowledge.” So Henry, *Quodlibet* IX q. 15, and Brother William of Ware, d. 3 of this first [book], in the question whether the soul always understands itself by an elicited act. Again, after his fourth argument to prove that there is production in God, he brings in the proofs of others. The first, “Here a certain doctor argues otherwise,” [argues] from the fact that a person is constituted by a relation. For the second, it is Henry who argues for production in God, *Quodlibet* VI q. 1, and the following argument is his too. Then follows: “Others argue from the nature of the good,” etc.; these are arguments of Bonaventure, book I, d. 2, q. 2. Having solved this third question, he next answers the fourth question, first setting out the opinion of Henry in the *Summa* and in *Quodlibet* VI q. 1, on account of the equality of essential and notional acts. Having finished the fourth question he begins to solve [the rest]: first by proving that there are two produced persons, second that there is one unproduced. “It remains now to prove that one is unproduced”: where he says “a certain doctor,” this doctor is Henry, *Quodlibet* VI q. 1. Last, he begins to solve the first question, and a little before he answers the principal arguments he says: “Here let that doctor’s saying about twofold predication be brought in.” This is Bonaventure, book I, d. 5, q. 1.

{#s015-03} But coming to the Doctor’s [[p. 28|v° · Di.ii. Q.iii]] opinion, he answers the third question first, saying yes: in the Father there are two distinct intrinsic principles, which require two distinct [things] produced inwardly. The first is clear, because in the Father there is fruitful memory, or intellect, through which he produces the Son. Second, concerning the will: the will, having an object actually known and presented to it, is of its own nature productive of perfect love of such an object, that is, of the Holy Spirit. The Doctor’s second argument for this conclusion: the object that is in the memory, that is, in the possible intellect, produces itself in understanding, that is, in the agent intellect. But it cannot produce itself, since according to Augustine there is nothing that begets itself; therefore it produces something other than itself, and this can only be the Son; therefore etc.

{#s015-04} But someone might ask here: you say that the essence together with the intellect or fruitful memory is the productive principle of the Word, and the fruitful will the principle of the Holy Spirit. How are the persons of the Son and the Holy Spirit produced through understanding and willing, since understanding and willing are immanent actions (*Ethics* VI), and an immanent action leaves nothing in what undergoes it? Yet in the production of the Son and the Holy Spirit something must be left. To this a certain [author] says that these powers are operative and productive. They are operative inasmuch as they understand and will, and as such they have only immanent operations. But they are productive inasmuch as they speak and spirate, and considered as productive they leave something in what undergoes [their action]. And therefore Scotus says in the first *Quodlibet* that the Son is produced not by understanding but by speaking.

{#s015-05} To the fourth question he replies that there are only two intrinsic productions there. He proves this much as in the third, chiefly because there are only two principles that have the formal character of productive [principles]. That there are only two is clear: one principle is inclined naturally and terminates in the Son, the other freely and terminates in the Holy Spirit. From this it can be gathered that multiplication does not come about through matter, against Saint Thomas, especially since there are three articles condemned at Paris by Lord Stephen against him. One says this: “Intelligences have no matter, therefore God cannot make several angels of the same species”: error. The second: “God cannot multiply individuals without matter under the same species”: error. The third: “Forms do not admit of division except according to matter”: error, unless it is understood of forms educed from the potency of matter. Against Saint Thomas on this point is the Commentator, *On the Soul* II, comments 2 and 3, and Avicenna, *Metaphysics* II. But let these be left for [Book] II, d. 3.

{#s015-06} To the second question, which asks about the trinity of persons in God, he says that there are only three. He proves it thus: [[p. 29|r° · Di.iii. Q.i]] there are only two produced persons and one producing; therefore three. The consequence holds by the [word] *only*; the antecedent is clear from the preceding proofs in this question.

{#s015-07} To the first question he answers affirmatively. For any nature that is communicable to several by identity [is so communicable], therefore the divine nature [is], since the good is communicative of itself, and the greater the good, the more so. This is confirmed: the divine nature is whatever in a nature, as nature, is a pure perfection. This pure perfection is communicable to several; therefore the divine nature is. The minor holds, because a pure perfection is that which, in whatever it is, it is better to be than not to be. And so for the essence it is better to be in persons than not. He says further that the essence and the person must have distinct aspects, beyond [the work of] the intellect, by which [the one] is communicable and [the other] incommunicable.

{#s015-08} Note, where it is said on the second proof of the major below, in the reply to the third question, that one can gather that in God there is no cause *ad intra*. The reason is that a cause is that upon whose being something else follows by nature; the [word] *something else* signifies an essential distinction, which does not occur *ad intra*. *Ad extra* there is indeed a cause of creatures, below, where he speaks to the fourth question. You will find in *On the Soul* II that material powers do not reflect upon themselves, but immaterial ones do: sight does not see itself, but the intellect does understand itself.

{#s015-09} Note that eight pages on you will find what formal identity is. From that text you will be able to extract that for things to be formally distinct five [conditions] are required. First, that one is not in the other as in a natural potency; and so act and potency do not differ formally. Second, that one is not in the other virtually; for this distinction is different from the formal. Third, that one is not in the other in a certain confused [way]. Fourth, that one is not in the other totally. Fifth, that one is not of the quidditative concept of the other, as the properties are related to the essence.

{#s015-10} To the argument at the beginning I say that it speaks of the solitude of the divine nature and not of the solitude of a person.
