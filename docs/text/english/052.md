{#s052-h .heading} [Second question.]

{#s052-01} The second question of the seventeenth distinction, where it is asked whether charity, or any habit whatever, is an active principle with respect to the act. It seems not: the Apostle, 1 Corinthians, says: nothing profits without infused charity. To the contrary, the act of acquired charity is praised in Scripture, 2 Maccabees 15.

{#s052-02} To the question it is said that it is. For a habit is that by which its possessor acts when he wills, according to the Commentator, *On the Soul* 3; but to act belongs to an agent; therefore the habit stands effectively in respect of the act.

{#s052-03} On this question there are several opinions. One is Henry's (where cited above), that infused habits must be spoken of in one way and acquired habits in another: for acquired habits are not causes of the substance of the act but causes of readiness in acting, whereas infused habits are total causes both of the substance of the act and of readiness in acting. He rejects it. There is another opinion, of Godfrey of Fontaines, [[p. 84|v° · Di.xvii. Q.ii]](where cited above, in the preceding question), who says that the habit is the cause of the intensity in the act and the power is the cause of the substance of the act, so that to two things in the effect, namely substance and intensity, two correspond in the cause, namely power and habit. He rejects it. There is another opinion which says that the habit has no action or actuality with respect to the act, but only serves to incline the power to elicit the act, and that the habit does not have such actuality. There is another opinion, that the habit is a partial cause with respect to the perfect act, so that the power and the habit are two partial causes integrating one total cause. But as to which is the nobler, it is said: the power; for when the power acts the habit co-acts, and not conversely. And this is a tenable opinion.

{#s052-04} But is acquired charity in us the principle of any meritorious act? To this it is said that it is. But about this four things will be seen. First, what infused charity is and what acquired charity is. Second, for what purpose acquired charity is posited in the soul. Third, for what purpose infused charity is posited in us. Fourth, whether a man existing in charity can know that he has infused charity.

{#s052-05} As to the first article, it is said that infused charity is a supernatural habit caused by God supernaturally, for instance in baptism or when a man receives the sacraments; but acquired charity is a habit acquired from frequent virtuous acts, as, if I frequently elicit an act of love, I acquire for myself one habit. As to the second article, the holy Doctor says that acquired charity is posited in us for receiving acts well. The Doctor rejects this and says that acquired charity is posited in us to co-cause, or to be a second cause; and so the power will be the first cause and charity the second cause with respect to the act, to the end that the soul, by means of charity, may perform the act of loving.

{#s052-06} As to the third article, it is said by some that infused charity is posited to co-cause the act. The Doctor rejects this and says that it is posited for the sake of the acceptance of our act, since without it none would be accepted, even supposing it were good in itself; and this is by divine ordination. As to the fourth, some answer negatively, since no one knows whether he is worthy of hatred or of love. But a certain one says that although we cannot know naturally that we have charity, since this is supernatural, yet we can know it by faith. For we hold by faith that to those who worthily and rightly receive the sacraments of the Church God infuses grace, as in bap[[p. 85|r° · Di.xviii. Q.i]]tism, [and] that to those who have intense love God forgives their sins.

{#s052-07} To the authority at the beginning, I say that the Apostle speaks of merit for eternal life; and in this way, according to the ordained laws, the power does not suffice without the habit of charity; and that is what he means.
