{#s060-h .heading} [Twenty-second distinction.]

{#s060-01} On the twenty-second distinction it is asked whether God can be named by man in the wayfaring state by some proper name. It seems so: for as God is understood, so he can be named by us; but we understand him under the notion of essence; therefore we can so name him. To the contrary: names presuppose the thing; but we do not know the thing; therefore neither the name.

{#s060-02} But after the arguments at the foot of the question is placed the opinion [[p. 96|v° · Di.xxii. Q.i]]of the Solemn Doctor in the *Summa*, and he says no — which the Doctor refutes in many ways elsewhere, etc. ‘From Henry's own sayings’ — for he says further below, where he touches the fourth part of the minor, when it is argued that it conflicts with his sayings elsewhere, inasmuch as he grants that a wayfarer can have some quidditative concept of God: Henry has this in the *Summa*, article 22, question 12 and 3. Again, quite close by, where the Doctor says, ‘Second, I argue thus: a perfect object can produce in the intellect,’ etc.; and immediately after, ‘if the divine essence, according to this doctor, cannot cause in the proper intellect more than a single real concept’: Henry has this in *Quodlibet* 5, question 1, and *Quodlibet* 13, question 1. And then he answers the question according to his own mind. It is true that this question can scarcely be found complete, unless it be completed either from the Paris reportations or from elsewhere.

{#s060-03} But note, before entering on the question, that some say there are certain essential names, certain personal, and certain notional names. Essential names are those which belong to all [the persons], like ‘essence’. Personal names are those which belong to one person only, like ‘Father’, ‘Son’. Notional names are those which belong to the act of one person, like ‘generation’, or of several but not all, like ‘spiration’.

{#s060-04} But coming to the question, it is said that God is nameable by us: Exodus 15 says, ‘The Lord is a man of war; Almighty is his name.’ Henry says, where cited above, that the divine nature, or God, can be named by a proper name by God alone. But Scotus says that for something to be named by someone happens in two ways: either by imposing a name on it or by using a name already imposed. And each of these modes is said in two ways, since one may impose a name perfectly or imperfectly; likewise one may use a name perfectly or imperfectly. Perfectly, as men use certain names, as ‘man’ is said from ‘humanity’; imperfectly, as when it is said, ‘the meadow laughs’.

{#s060-05} Second, he says that one can use a name in four ways. First, one uses a name as it is such a thing, as was said of ‘man’. Second, one uses a name as a sign at pleasure, without having a concept of the thing signified, but knowing only that it is a significant name — as if a Latin speaker were to name [something] with the Greek [word for] ‘man’. Third, one uses a name as a sign having a concept of the thing signified only in general, as that a man is a substance or an animal. Fourth, one uses a name as a sign expressing a concept distinctly and in particular.

{#s060-06} As to the matter in hand, he says that in the first three ways God is nameable by us; in the fourth way not. And the reason for these can be had thus: for just as of [[p. 97|r° · Di.xxiii. Q.i]]God we can have three quidditative concepts, so according to them we can impose three names on him: for we can say that he is being, substance, spirit. And just as we cannot have a specific quidditative [concept], neither can we impose a proper and special name. And so it must be said of the names of God — apply [it]. The argument at the beginning is clear from what has been said in the question.
