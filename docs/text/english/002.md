{#s002-t .heading} [[p. 3|r°]] Here happily begins the *Scotus pauperum* (“Scotus for the Poor”), in which the opinions of the doctors and of Scotus contained in the four books of the *Sentences* are elucidated; published at Toulouse by the eminent professor of arts and of sacred theology Guillermus Gorris of Aragon, for the benefit of the poor.

{#s002-01} Although the free licence to compose books increases their number without end, I nevertheless judged it worth the labour to condense the opinions of the most ancient authorities, set down at great and diffuse length, since the path through them is no short one: partly because, especially for men devoted to letters, ripeness of age and the end of natural life overtake them first; partly in consideration of the benefit of a number of those who travel; and partly, too, at the instigation of the most reverend father in Christ and most illustrious lord, Alfonso of Aragon, Archbishop of Zaragoza.

{#s002-02} For these reasons I persuaded myself to bring forward into the open the proper authors of the opinions which, in the four books of the most subtle doctor of sacred theology, appear to be handed down under a kind of cloud, so to speak. And I judged that, once the enigmas of all the opinions had been rooted out, the little stones and the flowers should be picked out, after the manner of parrots(?) or of bees, and laid up in one place as in a hive; so that what some attain only after no small space of time, by turning over very many books — nay, what most never attain at all, through want of confidence or a life too short — the rest, persevering, and especially those who travel, may come to possess, those same precious and honey-sweet opinions, within no long interval of time, by means of a single little book compiled from many.

{#s002-03} For the conclusions of arguments and apt abridgements of opinions bear no little fruit in themselves, if the former is governed by right understanding and the latter by a well-defined grasp of the mind.

{#s002-04} Nor would I wish these words to be thought arrogantly spoken. For although I concede to many the science of philosophy and theology, and have myself spent my life in that study, were I to claim it for myself I should seem to be laying claim to it, in some measure, as by my own right. Wherefore I earnestly exhort those who read this little work [[p. 4|v°]] that, if they find anything precious in it, they rightly deem it to be ascribed, first, to God most high, the illuminator of all things; next, to the reverend master John Scotus, the clarifier of this divine wisdom; and also to the aforesaid most reverend and most illustrious Archbishop, who spurred on these labours. Whatever deserves reproof I would have ascribed to my own insufficiency.

{#s002-05} Yet may the desire to know the truth, and the no slight love that I bear towards study and towards those who spurred on this little work — by which I am drawn and utterly compelled to set hand to pen — render me fully excused in these matters. For I do not approach this task moved by hope of gain or by glory, but so that my industry (if one may so speak) may satisfy the minds, first and foremost, of so very great a man, and thence of a number of others. Those, then, who praise this little work and strengthen it with better reasons, or who even justly reprove it, I shall embrace; those who disparage it I shall not resist, but, like a lamb (so to speak) set before the shearer, I shall not open my mouth.

{#s002-06} Nor indeed shall I omit to invoke the grace of divine favour, lest recourse be had to human aid before divine. Therefore I, Guillermus Gorris, the least among the masters of the sacred page, and unworthy withal, desiring that all things be begun, carried forward and finished to the praise and glory of almighty God, of the Virgin Mary His mother, and of the whole heavenly court, bending the knees — if not of the body, then at least of the heart — offer to God, as far as I am able, humble prayers: that, not weighing human demerits, His infinite clemency alone may pour His graces into me, supply my defect of knowledge and eloquence, and enlarge the small measure of my wit and the capacity of my memory, so that I may set forth compendiously the truth of the doctrine of Scotus, put errors to flight, unlock enigmas, untie knots, elucidate what is obscure and explain what is doubtful; and that thus this most sacred science may be planted, built up, strengthened and preserved in the minds of eager hearers, so that thereby their bodies may be the richer and the more honoured, and their minds may apply themselves the more readily to holy exhortation; and thus may their souls be instructed, adorned by God, and clothed with many virtues, so that they may merit beatitude.

{#s002-07} May this work, moreover, be a choice aide-mémoire for the advanced; for those beginning to ripen, a means of reaching the intended end more swiftly; and for the young, a way opened before them. And may it, once the sleep of negligence and diffidence has been shaken off, make the sluggish assiduous and wakeful in study, so that my discourse, finding in them a spark of love for this science, may flow into it and nourish it, and, when it has grown, make of it a most worthy flame.

{#s002-08} And although I believe that my words do not stray from the straight path of the doctrine of Scotus, being, as it were, subject to it, nevertheless with filial obedience I submit them to the correction and emendation of the aforesaid [[p. 5|r°]] most reverend and most illustrious lord Archbishop, and of anyone whose judgement is better than mine; and above all of the holy Roman Church, which is the mother and mistress of all the faithful; and I truly profess that I hold only what she approves. If, however, anything in this little work should run counter to catholic teaching, then, that I may escape the mark of censure, I here and now delete it from my heart, my mind and my book, and will that it be deleted.

{#s002-09} It seemed to me, however — as it did to the greatest of lawgivers — unseemly to approach the appointed matter straightaway without first rehearsing the naming and origin of this work. For such prefaces lead us the more willingly to the reading of the matter proposed, and, once we arrive there, afford a clearer understanding of it. It seems necessary to me, therefore, first of all to set out the origin and the name of this little work. And indeed, since I have here gathered together the polished opinions of all four books of Scotus, I consider that this rivulet draws its origin immediately from him, as from a fountain. Therefore I think that its name should not be claimed for myself but attributed to Scotus himself; and its title shall be *Scotus pauperum*, “Scotus for the Poor”, for the poor will “scotize” the more easily with him.

{#s002-10} But let me not pass over the division of the work in silence, for it rouses the mind of the reader, prepares the understanding of one who would understand, and artfully refreshes the memory. In each question, then, I shall observe the following order. First, I shall recite the opinions of the doctors, through which a way is opened to those who read Scotus. Second, the opinion of the Subtle Doctor, through which true theology is demonstrated. Third, the subtleties of the Doctor, through which whoever understands them is himself called “subtle”. In reciting the opinions, however, I have followed the articles of Scotus, so that every reader may know where the opinion in question is touched upon and by which doctor it is handed down. **In the course of the text, however, I have often set out the articles otherwise than as they are found in Scotus.** And I have done this so that the matter might be the more easily unravelled, and so that the students — my great love for whom makes me solicitous — might “scotize” the sooner.

{#s002-11} The overall division of the whole present work, as handed down by Scotus, is this. There being four books: in the first is treated the wondrous splendour of the divine generation, namely the origin of the Son from the Father, and of the Holy Spirit from both; in the second, the wondrous variety of the work produced, namely the new going-forth of the creature from its Creator; in the third, the wondrous kindness of the covenant of union, namely the union of God and man in our Redeemer; in the fourth, the wondrous utility of the gift bestowed, namely of the grace given in the administration or reception of the sacraments, and the glorification of soul and body in the blessed resurrection.
