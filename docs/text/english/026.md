{#s026-h .heading} [Eleventh question.]

{#s026-01} The eleventh question of the third distinction, which asks whether there is an image of the Trinity in the mind. It seems not: what is proper to the Word does not belong to a creature; but to be the image is proper to the Word; therefore etc. To the contrary, Genesis 1: “Let us make man to our image.”

{#s026-02} To the question he says that there is, from *On the Trinity* XIV, by Augustine, who says: the image is to be sought where there is nothing better in our nature; but there is nothing better in us than the soul or mind; therefore the image of the Trinity is in the mind. In this question the Doctor raises two doubts. In solving the first he touches on certain people who say that the Father has fruitfulness for producing the Holy Spirit only [as] [[p. 43|r° · Di.iii. Q.xi]] left over from the production of the Son: this is Henry, *Quodlibet* VI q. 2. Afterwards, in replying to the second argument, he says: “if the Father were held to beget as understanding, as one opinion holds”; this is Henry, *Quodlibet* VI q. 1.

{#s026-03} But for a fuller explanation we shall consider four things: first, what an image is; second, with respect to what in God it is [an image]; third, how it is in the mind in us, and in which acts; fourth, whether the things in which it consists differ really from one another and from the essence of the soul.

{#s026-04} As to the first: following the way of Thomas, *Summa* I q. 93 a. 6, it is said that an image represents distinctly under the aspect of the individual, whereas a vestige represents under the aspect of the species. The Doctor rejects this and says otherwise: an image represents the whole, a vestige represents a part. But this is not enough, because according to Augustine, *Eighty-three Questions*, if there were two similar eggs, one would represent the other, and yet one would not be the image of the other; for according to Augustine in the same place, an image follows that of which it is the image by imitation. And so an image represents the whole by imitation of that of which it is the image; but one egg is not so related to the other.

{#s026-05} As to the second, namely with respect to what in God the image in the mind is: it must be understood that the Trinity constitutes in our intellect a concept which is a kind of numerable whole, of which whole the divine persons are understood as quasi-parts. Hence the concept of one person is, in our intellect, partial with respect to the concept of the whole Trinity. The image in us, therefore, is not considered with respect to one person, nor with respect to that in which they are one, but with respect to the whole Trinity and One. That, then, which leads us to the knowledge of the Trinity and Unity by way of imitation will represent [it] as regards the total concept which our intellect can have of it: it will represent the distinction of the three persons, the unity of the essence, and the order of origin, since the real distinction in God is by origin. It will also have an imitation or essential order to the Trinity itself which it represents.

{#s026-06} As to the third, in what the image in us consists: it is said that the soul has in itself a certain perfection according to which it is first act with respect to begotten knowledge, and this is called memory; it also has in itself another perfection according to which it formally receives begotten knowledge, and this is called intelligence; and it has in itself a certain perfection according to which it receives volitions, and this is called will. Taking, then, these three on the side of [[p. 44|v° · Di.iiii. Q.i]] the soul as they stand under their three acts: in these, insofar as there is one substantiality for the three by reason of those three realities that are on the side of the soul, it is called an image.

{#s026-07} As to the fourth, there is the opinion of Thomas, *Summa* I q. 77 a. 8, who says that the powers of the soul in which the image of the Trinity consists are distinct really from one another and from the essence of the soul, flowing from it as an accident from its subject, yet are inseparable accidents. But this opinion seems to me to be against Augustine, *On the Trinity* X, ch. 11: “These three, memory, intelligence and will, since they are not three lives but one life, nor three minds but one mind, consequently are certainly not three substances but one substance.” And throughout the whole chapter he proves this at length. And so, if they are one, they are not really distinct; and if [they are] substance, [they are] not accidents, against St Thomas.

{#s026-08} But suppose someone says: the powers must be related to one another, for there to be an image, as the persons are; but the persons in God are really distinct; therefore so are the powers. The Doctor says, in II d. 15 at the end, that the image is to be considered in the powers by comparison with the acts, which acts are really distinct. And so the image is virtually in the soul, and formally as it stands under its second acts. To the argument at the beginning it is said that it is proper to the Word to be the image of the Father; but the image of the Trinity is another matter.
