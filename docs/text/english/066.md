{#s066-h .heading} [Twenty-seventh distinction.]

{#s066-01} On the twenty-seventh distinction it is asked whether the word of a created mind is the actual act of understanding. It seems not; for Augustine says: the voice is the vehicle of the mental word, but it is not the sign of the act of understanding, but of the thing. To the contrary is Augustine in the same place: that the word is knowledge itself.

{#s066-02} To this question Henry replies, in Quodlibet 6, q. 1, and in the *Summa*, that the word is the declarative act of understanding.

{#s066-03} In this question one must see whether the word that sounds outwardly, that is, the sensible word, principally signifies the concept of the mind or the thing outside. Secondly, the question will be answered.

{#s066-04} On the first there are four ways of speaking. One, that the sensible word principally signifies the concept of the mind, because the Philosopher says in *Peri hermeneias* 1 that spoken sounds are signs of the passions that are in the soul. Again, Augustine in the *Enchiridion* says that names were imposed to signify the compositions of minds. Again, the sensible word signifies that in which there is truth or falsity, and so what is understood. Another opinion, that the sensible word principally signifies the thing outside; otherwise no proposition would be true. Another opinion, that the word has a twofold relation, one to the speaker, the other to the hearer: with respect to the speaker it signifies the concept of the mind; with respect to the hearer it signifies the thing [continued]
