{#s059-h .heading} [Twenty-first distinction.]

{#s059-01} On the twenty-first distinction it is asked whether this proposition is true: ‘The Father alone is God.’ It seems so: for this is true, ‘God alone is the Father’; therefore the Father alone is God. The antecedent is clear through its exponents; the consequence is proved by the conversion of the exclusive. To the contrary is Augustine, *On the Trinity* 6, chapter 8.

{#s059-02} In the reply to the first article, where it is said, ‘Some also distinguish the principal proposition of the question, as if this, “The Father alone is God”, could have a false sense in composition and a true sense in division, adducing that [saying] of Priscian,’ etc.: this is taken from Brother William of Ware, in his third [book], distinction 7, the single question, and from St Bonaventure, the third literal doubt on the fourth distinction of this first [book].

{#s059-03} But coming to the question, it is said that it is not true, as is had from Augustine, *On the Trinity* 6. But three things will be seen. First, whether one relative belongs to the primary concept of the other. Second, whether an exclusive word added to one relative excludes the other. Third, concerning the principal question.

{#s059-04} As to the first, the view is that of Giles, book 1, distinction 21: he says that one relative does not belong to the primary [[p. 95|r° · Di.xxii. Q.i · sig. g.i]]concept of the other, but to the secondary. But against this: of things that are simultaneous in nature and simultaneous in the intellect, one belongs to the primary concept of the other; but relatives are such, according to the Philosopher in the *Categories*; therefore one relative belongs to the primary concept of the other.

{#s059-05} As to the second, it is said that, although one relative belongs to the primary concept of the other, still, if an exclusive word is added to one relative, it excludes the other. For, as is said above, one relative belongs to the primary concept of the other, sometimes not as the same but as other.

{#s059-06} As to the third article, it is said that this proposition, ‘The Father alone is God,’ is not true. For the evidence of which I premise three rules about exclusive words, and from them the point will be proved in three ways. The first is this: that an affirmative exclusive implies a universal affirmative with the terms transposed. An example: when it is said, ‘Socrates alone runs,’ it implies this: ‘Everything running is Socrates.’ The second rule: that an affirmative exclusive with a finite predicate implies a universal negative with an infinite predicate. An example: when it is said, ‘Socrates alone runs,’ it implies this: ‘No non-Socrates runs,’ or this: ‘No one other than Socrates runs.’ The third rule: that an affirmative exclusive has two propositions to expound it, of which one is affirmative and the other negative; and where either of those exponents is false, it follows that the proposition expounded is false.

{#s059-07} Then from the first rule it is had that this is false: ‘The Father alone is God.’ It is clear: for then this would be true, ‘Every God is the Father’ — which it is not. By the second rule too it is false, namely, ‘The Father alone is God; therefore no one other than the Father is God’; but this is false; therefore the first is. By the third rule too it is false, namely, ‘The Father alone is God.’ It is expounded thus: ‘The Father is God, and no one other than the Father is God’; but one of the exponents is false; therefore the exclusive is false. And so it is concluded that this is false: ‘The Father alone is God.’

{#s059-08} To the argument at the beginning, I say that the exclusive is not converted: for the Philosopher does not say that it is converted; or else a fallacy is committed in it.
