{#s025-h .heading} [Tenth question.]

{#s025-01} The tenth question of the third distinction, which asks whether the principal cause of begotten knowledge is the object, present in itself or in its species, or the intellective part of the soul itself. It seems that it is the object: for that is more principal which moves without being moved; but the object moves without being moved, whereas the soul is moved, being moved by the object, as is had in *On the Soul* III, and the intellect does not move unless moved; therefore etc. To the contrary: the universal cause concurs principally, as is clear of the sun and man (*Physics* II); but the intellect is a universal cause, the object a particular one, for through the object the intellect is determined to understanding this or that; therefore etc.

{#s025-02} In this question there are no opinions. But coming to the point, it is said that the intellective part has the more principal causality with respect to the intellections that belong to us naturally in the present state. The reason is this: when causes are so related that one is unlimited, that is, common, while the other is determined by the utmost of its power to a definite effect, the one that is unlimited, or more unlimited and more universal, seems to be the more principal and more perfect, as in the example of the sun and of particular things that are generated and generate. So are the intellect and the object related: for the intellect has an unlimited and common power with respect to all intelligibles, but the objects we know naturally have a determinate power. Therefore the intellect will be the more principal cause.

{#s025-03} [[p. 42|v° · Di.iii. Q.xi]] Further he says that it is especially to be noted that some object exceeding the capacity of our intellective part, for instance the beatific object as clearly seen, can have the whole causality with respect to the vision, or a more principal [causality] than the intellective part; and this because of the excellence of such an object and the deficiency of the intellective part.

{#s025-04} But for intellection a created light is required, or the agent intellect, which I take to be the same thing. And it is said that this is so: *On the Soul* III, it is necessary to posit in the nature of the soul a twofold power, one of which is to make all things, the other to become all things. But for what purpose is the agent intellect posited? I say: to co-cause many things that are posited or caused in the possible intellect. For the agent intellect contains in itself four effects essentially ordered. First, it co-causes with the phantasm, in the possible intellect, the intelligible species, which the phantasm alone cannot cause. The second effect is the act of understanding itself, which it causes together with the intelligible species. The third effect is the disposition to the habit, which it causes, though not together with the act. The fourth effect is the habit, which it likewise causes together with the species. And as has been said of the agent intellect in its efficient causality of these, so must it be said of the possible intellect in its receptive causality, for it receives all the aforesaid in the same way.

{#s025-05} But supposing it is asked by what that species is conveyed from the common sense to the imagination, from the imagination to the estimative power, and from the estimative to the memory, etc., it is said that there are certain spirits that carry it from cell to cell. To the argument at the beginning, I say that while it receives species the intellect is moved, being itself moved; yet when it goes out into the act of understanding it moves without being moved. Or it is said that it is the principal cause in operating, though not in moving.
