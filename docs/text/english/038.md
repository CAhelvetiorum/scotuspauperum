{#s038-h .heading} [Ninth distinction.]

{#s038-01} On the ninth distinction, whether the generation of the Son in God is eternal. It seems that the generation of the Son is not eternal: for it is said in the Psalm, “Before the day-star I begot thee”; “before” is a difference of time; therefore etc. To the contrary, it is had in the book of Wisdom: “From the beginning and before the ages I was created”; and Proverbs, chapter 8.

{#s038-02} In this question, in answering the third argument, he quotes Gregory on that [verse] of Job, “Didst thou know then that thou shouldst be born?” Here it must be known that, in truth, the authority of Gregory taken from the *Moralia*, book 29, which the Subtle Doctor says Gregory spoke on that [verse] of Job 38, “Didst thou know then that thou shouldst be born?”, is not on that phrase, but on the phrase of the same chapter of Job, and of the same book in the *Moralia*, which the Lord had said to Job a little before: “Didst thou since thy birth command the morning, and show the dawn its place?” Hence it must be said that the Doctor meant “on that [verse] of Job”, i.e. before that [verse] of Job.

{#s038-03} But leaving these things and coming to the purpose, I shall say four things. First, under what aspect generation is posited in God. Second, whether that generation is eternal. Third, whether the eternity of the Father and of the Son is the same. Fourth, whether in that eternity there is any priority of the Father to the Son.

{#s038-04} As to the first, it is said that generation in creatures implies two things, namely change and production. These have different formal *rationes*, separable from one another: for change is the act of a mutable subject, which passes from potency to act, as is plain from *Physics* VI; production, however, belongs formally to the thing produced, to which it is accidental that it come about with change. To the point, I say that generation is not posited in God under the aspect of change, because as such it implies imperfection, owing to the potentiality attached to it; and nothing such is to be posited in God. Generation is therefore posited in God under the aspect of production, as it is the term of what is produced, and not under the aspect of change, as it is the subject of what is changed.

{#s038-05} [[p. 63|r° · Di.ix. Q.i · sig. e.i]] As to the second, I say that the generation of the Son is eternal. This is plain from Ambrose, *On the Trinity* I, chapter 6, and it is set down in the text: “If the Father was earlier, and afterwards begot, he was changed by the accession of generation. God forbid such madness! He therefore always had the Son.” Likewise the authority of Hilary in the text: between begetting and being begotten there is no middle, namely of duration. If therefore it is proper to the Father always to have begotten, it is proper to the Son that he is always begotten. Therefore he says that, as generation is not there under the aspect of change, as was said in distinction 5, question 2, so it does not have terms corresponding to the terms of the generation [that is] change, namely being after non-being: for all things are there in the same now of eternity.

{#s038-06} As to the third, I say that the eternity of the Father and of the Son is numerically the same. The reason is this: things that have one being of existence have one duration, since duration regards the being of existence; but the Father and the Son have one being of existence (I do not say of subsistence); therefore they have one duration, or eternity.

{#s038-07} As to the fourth, there are some, namely the Thomists, who say that among the divine persons there is order, yet not with prior and posterior, since they are founded on what is indivisible. The opinion of the Doctor is that priority can be taken in five ways: of duration, of nature, of perfection, of reason and of origin. In God, however, the first three priorities do not occur, as is plain from Augustine, in the book *On the Faith, to Peter*, who, speaking of the three divine persons, says: “None of them precedes another in eternity, or exceeds in greatness, or surpasses in power.” Yet priority of reason and of origin is posited in the Father with respect to the Son, because acting presupposes being; but the Father produces the Son; therefore in some way, by reason or by origin, the Father is presupposed to the Son. By this priority of origin, however, I understand nothing else than that from which another is, while it itself is from none, as the Son is from the Father, and the Father from no one.

{#s038-08} But note, for the whole question, that verbs of any tense are said of God, and truly, whether they signify personal or essential acts. This is plain from Augustine on John, homily 18, on that [verse] of John 16: “For he shall not speak of himself, but what things soever he shall hear, he shall speak.” “He shall hear”, says Augustine of the Spirit, “because he has indeed heard, he hears”; because for the Holy Spirit to hear is to proceed from the Father and the Son; and consequently what he hears he will hear and has heard, just as now he knows, will know and has known. He therefore means that verbs of all tenses are truly said of God; and this appears from what the Master adduces, distinction 8, chapter 2. But what do these verbs of different tenses signify when they are said of God? The answer is that they can more properly be called [[p. 64|v° · Di.x. Q.i]] co-signifying the now of eternity than differences of time. To the argument at the beginning it is said that “before” is not there put for a difference of time, but is understood [as] “before every creature” or “from eternity” etc.
