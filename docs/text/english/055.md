{#s055-h .heading} [Third question.]

{#s055-01} The third question of the eighteenth distinction, where it is asked whether charity is increased by the extraction of a new part from potency to act. It seems so: for if charity is intensified, then it is changed, and a new part is drawn from the potency of the preceding [charity] into act, since to be intensified includes to be changed. To the contrary: for according to Boethius, a simple form cannot be a subject.

{#s055-02} But where he says, ‘One master says three things here’: here the master is Henry, *Quodlibet* 5, question 19. And he concludes that augmentation comes about by the extraction of degrees.

{#s055-03} But to the question the Doctor answers no. For if charity were increased by extraction or eduction from potency to act, then either it is drawn from the potency of the subject or from that of the pre-existing charity. Not in the first way, since then it would be a form naturally educed, and so a na[[p. 89|r° · Di.xix. Q.i]]tural form, which is false and against the faith. Nor in the second way, since the preceding and the following form of charity are forms of the same nature; therefore one is not a material principle with respect to the other, from which it might be educed.

{#s055-04} But, this question having been determined, one could ask whether charity is augmentable to infinity. And about this three things will be done. First, two sayings will be set down. Second, I shall adduce three conclusions concerning the matter. Third, I shall say whether the divine intellect produces charity in cognized being.

{#s055-05} As to the first, I set down two sayings. The first, that charity is not augmentable to infinity according to parts of the same quantity, is clear: for every quantity is terminable and exhaustible, and so cannot go to infinity. The second saying is that it is augmentable to infinity according to parts of the same proportion. This is clear: for by these no increase or diminution comes about.

{#s055-06} As to the second article, I set down three conclusions. The first, that conjoined charity is not augmentable to infinity, is clear: for it would exceed the soul in which it exists, since the soul is not augmentable to infinity. The second conclusion: that separate charity is augmentable to infinity. This is clear, since, given any multitude of rational souls whatever, God can cause another and another charity in them, and so to infinity. The third conclusion: that charity can be infinite in act or in potency; or rather, not in act but certainly in potency; or, on the strength of the two preceding conclusions, that it is augmentable to infinity according to parts of the same proportion and not according to parts of the same quantity. Others say, and well, concerning these three conclusions, that since the rational soul is finite, an infinite charity cannot be given in it, but certainly a charity finite [and increasable] to infinity.

{#s055-07} As to the third, it is said that our intellect produces its objects in cognized being, which I show thus. A knowable object is in potency to being known, like a rose. But if the intellect did not produce its object in cognized being, it would not be a knowable object in act, since it is never known except through the intellect, etc. But if this must be said of our intellect, then also of the divine intellect.

{#s055-08} To the argument at the beginning, it is said that it does not follow, ‘charity is intensified, therefore charity is changed’: for only the subject is changed.
