{#s030-h .heading} [Second question.]

{#s030-01} The second question of the fifth distinction, where it is asked whether the Son is begotten of the substance of the Father. It seems not, because Augustine, *On the Trinity* V, ch. 33, says: “we do not say three persons out of the same substance.” Again, substance seems to stand uniformly to each person; therefore no person is of the substance of another. To the contrary is Augustine, *On the Trinity*, ch. 19.

{#s030-02} At the beginning of the solution is the opinion of Henry, *Quodlibet* VIII q. 9 and *Summa* art. 54 q. 3. Later, at the place “To this opinion it is added by those, etc.”: this is Godfrey. Next, where he argues against the holder of that opinion with the fourth authority, Augustine *Against Maximinus*, and introduces the expression about begetting and being begotten ‘by impression’: on this see Henry, *Quodlibet* VI q. 1. And it is Henry’s opinion and determination that the Son of God is of the substance of the Father as of a matter.

{#s030-03} But answering the question, it is the view of Augustine, *Against Maximinus* III: “You will in no way know the Son of God if you deny that he is of the substance of the Father.” But on Henry’s opinion it is said that in Augustine’s time no one dared to posit matter or quasi-matter in God. The reason is this. Generation in creatures says two things, namely change and production, and their formal notions are separable from each other: for production is the form of the thing produced, and it is accidental to it that it occurs with change; while change is the act of a changeable subject which passes from privation to form. These two are joined in the generation of creatures, and the reason is the imperfection of the created agent, which always needs something to be acted upon. If a created begetter were a perfect agent, it would not need matter from which or upon which to act, but could produce its effect by itself, and then there would be production without change. Since, then, in God there is nothing of imperfection, there must there be production [[p. 49|r° · Di.v. Q.ii]] of what is perfect, without change, and consequently without matter.

{#s030-04} But that this may be better explained, four conclusions are set out. First: the Son is not of the substance of the Father *because* he is from him by origination. Proof: if the Son were of the Father’s substance for this reason, then a creature would be of the Father’s substance, because it is from him by origination. Second conclusion: the Son is not of the substance of the Father *because* he is consubstantial with the Father; for since the Father is consubstantial with the Son, he would be of the substance of the Son, which the doctors do not grant. Third conclusion: the Son is said to be of the Father’s substance by these two together, namely because he originates from him and is consubstantial with him. Likewise one says “the Son is of the substance of the Father,” and it is not the same to say “the Son is consubstantial with the Father,” because the Father is consubstantial with the Son, and yet the Father is not of the substance of the [Son]. Fourth conclusion: the Father and the Son are distinguished by origination and by consubstantiality, but principally by origination rather than by consubstantiality, leaving aside here what constitutes them.

{#s030-05} What, then, does “the Son is of the substance of the Father” mean? It is said: that the Son originates from the Father and is consubstantial with him. What, then, does ‘of’ express? It is said: not matter, nor anything having the role of matter. But someone might ask how relation and essence make a person, if the essence is not matter and does not have the role of matter. It is said that if it had the role of matter, then essence and relation would constitute the person; but this is false, because the person is not constituted or composite but simple. This is because that notion is perfectly the same as it, owing to the infinity, which is of one notion. The perfection of identity, therefore, excludes all composition.

{#s030-06} From this note that the divine essence is communicated not as form to matter but as quiddity to supposit. Note also that the divine relations are in the divine essence not by informing it but as subsisting in a nature. Note also, near the end of the question, that relation and essence are in the person in such a way that neither is a form informing the other; rather they are perfectly the same, though not formally. But is the essence, as prescinding from the will, a productive principle of anything *ad extra* by way of efficient cause? I say not: for if the essence were an efficient cause, since the will is the first rule of contingent things, if these two concurred then the essence would be subordinated to the will and would be less perfect, which is false. To the argument at the beginning it is said that Augustine gives the reason in the same place: that substance is one thing there and person another. And so I grant that it is not properly said that some person is of the essence taken absolutely, but only by adding to the substance some originating person; and in this way it is granted that the Son is of the substance of the Father.
