{#s037-h .heading} [Fifth question.]

{#s037-01} The fifth question of the eighth distinction, where it is asked whether God alone is immutable. It seems that he is not immutable: for in Genesis, chapter 6, it is written of God, “touched inwardly with sorrow of heart”; therefore he is changed. To the contrary, Malachi 3 says: “I am God, and I change not” etc.

{#s037-02} In the second article, after ten modes have been compared, of which three suffice for the purpose, the opinion of Henry is set out, *Quodlibet* VIII, question 9, in which ten modes are posited. Henry posits ten modes of a thing's existing, in *Quodlibet* VIII, question 9. The first is of that which is of itself necessary being and from no one by way of principle, as God the Father. The second is of that which is necessary being but from another by way of principle, not by way of cause, as the Son of God and the Holy Spirit. The third is what is necessary being but from another by way of cause. The fourth is what is necessary being, yet not from another by way of principle or of cause, but dependently. The fifth is what is of itself possible being, yet from another necessary being. The sixth is of that which is of itself possible being and is not from another, yet is necessary being. The seventh, of that which is in itself possible, also from another by way of cause, yet from eternity. The eighth is what is in itself possible and from another by way of cause, but temporally, with the whole of time. The ninth, what is in itself possible and from another by way of cause, but with a part of time. The tenth, of that which is possible being neither of itself nor from another.

{#s037-03} The same [author's] *Quodlibet* VI, last question, helps with this matter, where he is content with four modes. The first is that the orbs receive only motion from the First, but not being. The second, that they are of themselves necessary being but from another by way of cause. The third, that the orbs are of themselves possible being but from another necessary being; and this is had from Avicenna. The fourth, that the orbs are of themselves possible being and from another: and this is the opinion of Plato and of the faith, because God produces contingently and conserves contingently. Hence it is that in our Doctor it is sometimes written “in ten modes”, following *Quodlibet* VIII, question 9, and sometimes “in four modes”, following *Quodlibet* VI, last question.

{#s037-04} And when the Doctor says, “This is confirmed by the confirmation of the argument”, [the argument] is adduced by Henry in the *Summa*, article 54, question 3, in the third principal [argument]. And when it is said in the text against this conclusion, on which the philosophers commonly agree, that the first cause necessarily and naturally causes the first effect, this is Henry's argument, questions 5 and 4. The second argument is set against [[p. 61|r° · Di.viii. Q.v]] the philosophers: “because a power regarding etc.” The reply is Henry's, in the same place, where he says: “This argument, although in itself somewhat plausible…”; it is [the argument] of the Doctor against Henry, as though [Henry] contradicted himself by what was said above.

{#s037-05} But coming to the question, as to the exclusive [word], since it is asked whether God *alone* [is immutable], the answer is that nothing other than God is immutable, speaking of the immutability that is [opposed to] turning, because nothing else is formally necessary. Anything else, too, is subjectively mutable, unless because of a negative imperfection: for instance, the last accident, which is capable of no perfection because of its own imperfection; as, if there is some relation, it is not subjectively mutable, because it cannot be the subject of any perfection; that is to say, because it is negatively imperfect, i.e. not capable of any perfection. But nothing other than God is immutable because of its perfection: for if anything is such, it would most of all be the first Intelligence. But that is mutable from intellection to intellection. I prove it: because it can have an intellection of any intelligible whatever, since our intellect can have this; but not a single [intellection] of all, because then that would be infinite, from distinction 2, question 1; nor infinitely many intellections of all intelligibles at once, because then an intellect having these at once and distinctly in act would seem to be infinite. Therefore it will be able to have an intellection of one intelligible after the intellection of another intelligible; and so it will be changed, or will be mutable, which we do not attribute to God.

{#s037-06} But in this question it is posited [as a further issue] whether God acts of necessity. And he sets out the opinions of the philosophers, then of the theologians, where he concludes that God does not act of necessity. For many unfitting consequences would follow. First, that nothing would be caused contingently. Second, that there would be no evil in the universe. Third, that secondary causes would do nothing. Fourth, that all things would be one. Fifth, that they would move in no time. Sixth, that there would be no generation or corruption in lower things; and so no resurrection either.

{#s037-07} Note, before he says, “As to the second principal [argument]”, where it is said that to whatever the act is repugnant, the potency is repugnant, [that] this is to be understood of natural things and not of voluntary ones. For the blessed one who sees God will not sin, and the act is repugnant to him, but not the potency, as the Doctor holds in [Book] II, distinction 7, and in [Book] IV, in the matter of beatitude. Here you also have why the will wills this rather than that. He says: because the will is the will; or, that it belongs to the undisciplined to seek the causes of all things (*Metaphysics* IV); [[p. 62|v° · Di.ix. Q.i]] or, because God's willing is the principle in contingent things, and nothing is prior to it in operating, although the essence is prior in being. Here you further have that by an ancient will a new effect can come about without change of will. But for what reason, if God wills something, does he always will it? It is said that this is so because he is not composed of a rational and an animal part as we are; therefore he always enjoys a single and simple pleasure (*Ethics* VII, at the end). To the argument at the beginning it is said that there is no change on God's part, but only on the part of the object.
