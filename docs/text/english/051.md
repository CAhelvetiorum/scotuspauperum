{#s051-h .heading} [Seventeenth distinction.]

{#s051-01} On the seventeenth distinction it is asked whether one must posit in the mind a created charity inhering in the nature capable of beatitude. It seems not: for the Apostle says, ‘the charity of God is poured forth in your hearts by the Holy Spirit.’ To the contrary, the same Apostle: ‘The fruit of the Holy Spirit is charity.’

{#s051-02} In this distinction he raises two questions together. And having set out the arguments on either side, answering first the second question, he sets forth the opinion of Henry, *Quodlibet* 4, question 10, who distinguishes between infused habit and acquired habit. And he says that virtue is according to that notion of virtue which is set down in *Ethics* 2, that virtue is what perfects its possessor and renders his work good; but that theological virtue does not behave so. In some books it is found that in *Ethics* 2 there is ‘virtue is the uttermost of a power’; but such a text is not found there, but is in *On the Heavens* 1. And so some books are corrupt here, since that text is not rightly cited as *Ethics* 2, but as *On the Heavens* 1.

{#s051-03} Afterwards is set down the opinion of Godfrey of Fontaines, who says that the act has its substance from the power, but this or that intensity from the habit (*Quodlibet* 11, question 4). It is the opinion of the Master that charity is not something created in the soul, but that the Holy Spirit in a special way moves the soul to the meritorious act; but this opinion is rejected by the theologians.

{#s051-04} The truth of the question is that it is necessary to posit created charity in the soul, according to Augustine, *On the Morals of the Church*, chapter 4, treating that verse of Romans 4 [*recte* 8]: ‘Who shall separate you from the charity of Christ?’ ‘Charity,’ he says, ‘of God: this is called virtue.’ But for fuller clarification three things will be set down. First, whether it is necessary, by the ordained power of God, to posit created charity in our soul. Second, whether God can by his absolute power accept someone without charity. Third, whether he necessarily loves a man existing in charity.

{#s051-05} As to the first, it is said that it is necessary to posit in us created charity in the soul in order that it be beatified, which is clear. For the sinner before penance is unworthy of eternal life; after penance he is worthy of eternal life; therefore [[p. 83|r° · Di.xvii. Q.ii · sig. f.2]]something then inheres that did not inhere before. But this is not faith nor hope, since these were in the sinner before his conversion; therefore charity is inhering there, and so the proposition is had. But of this we have no proper demonstration; but it is had through that text of John: ‘grace and truth came by Jesus.’ Again, in every habitual power one must necessarily posit a habit with respect to the subject; but our will is a habitual power; therefore etc. But such a habit is created charity; therefore one must posit charity in the soul.

{#s051-06} As to the second article, it is said that God by his absolute power can accept someone whom he had not accepted before, with no change made in the one accepted. For the divine will is no less able to accept what it did not accept before than our will is. But our will can accept one whom it did not accept before, with no change made in the one accepted; therefore the divine will can. That our will can is clear: for I can love you, with no change made in you, for love of you or of your friends.

{#s051-07} As to the third article, it is said that he does not necessarily love a man existing in charity: for if that were so of necessity, he would glorify the man, and moreover as quickly as he could; but this is false, since he accepts us freely to eternal life. To the argument at the beginning, and to the reasons that are adduced by the Master in this matter, I say that it is not understood that the charity poured forth or given is the Holy Spirit, but it is understood that it is poured forth or given by the Holy Spirit.
