{#s014-h .heading} [Second question.]

{#s014-01} The second question of the second distinction, where it is asked whether there is only one God. It seems not, 1 Corinthians 8: “For there are many gods and many lords.” To the contrary, Deuteronomy 6: “Hear, O Israel, the Lord your God is one”; and Isaiah 45: “Besides me there is no God.”

{#s014-02} Where it is said in this question, “The conclusion is certain,” the opinion of Brother William of Ware, book I, d. 2, q. 2, is given, namely that the unity of God is received from the law and therefore cannot be demonstrated. Rabbi Moses is of this mind. Likewise, in solving the last argument, concerning several goods, he also introduces this distinction, q. 2, following the third argument.

{#s014-03} But the Doctor proves that God is one by seven ways. The first is from infinity. If there were two gods, each would be infinite. The infinite must be known through its own essence; so the second god, knowing the other through the other’s essence, would not be infinite, since God must know all things through his own essence and not through the essence of another. The second way is from infinite will, thus: an infinite will is right; therefore it loves every lovable thing as much as it is lovable in itself. If so, it would love the second god infinitely, and consequently more than itself or equally, which is impossible. The third way is from infinite goodness. The will can desire a greater good; but if there were several infinite and several good and supremely good [beings], the will would not rest in one good, which is impossible. The fourth way is from infinite power, thus: in the same order there are not several total causes of the same effect; but infinite power is a total cause; therefore there are no other causes. The fifth way is from infinity, thus: the infinite cannot be exceeded; rather, it takes all things into itself. But if the infinite good were in several, either it would be exceeded or it would not take the other infinite into itself; therefore there are not several. [[p. 27|r° · Di.ii. Q.iii]] The sixth way is from necessary being. If there are two gods, they have two aspects of necessary being. Since they agree in a third thing, namely in essence, and in both that is necessary being, each god will have two necessary beings, which is unfitting, since in God there is only one necessary being. The seventh way is from the nature of omnipotence, thus: if God is omnipotent, he can make anything other than himself be and not be. So if one god made another, he could also destroy him, which is false.

{#s014-04} To the argument at the beginning it is said that the Apostle is speaking of idolaters, and therefore of gods in name only; for he adds: “but for us there is one God.”
