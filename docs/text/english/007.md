{#s007-01} The fifth and last question of the Prologue, namely whether theology is a practical science. It seems not, because Boethius, in *On the Trinity*, assigns three parts of speculative [science], one of which is theology. To the contrary, Romans 13: “The end of the law is love”; but love pertains to the practical; therefore theology is practical.

{#s007-02} Here [the Doctor] raises two questions at once, directing his eye to the final cause of theology: the first is particular, the second more general. Accordingly, having laid down a threefold division of articles, the third article is: from what does practical knowledge receive such extension? And it begins: “On the third article there are opinions.” One is this, namely that of Godfrey of Fontaines, *Quodlibet* X, q. 2; when it has been most subtly refuted, an evasion is subjoined: “To the arguments of the Doctor in the first paragraph, to the first, that of practical science”, etc. And this is the reply of the same Godfrey in the place cited. Having set this evasion aside, he brings forward a second opinion on the same point, there: “Otherwise it is said that that from which acts and habits are called practical is the end.” This is the opinion of the Solemn Doctor, *Quodlibet* VIII, q. 1; but it is refuted by the Doctor in many ways.

{#s007-03} The second question follows, and an opinion on it is subjoined where he puts it: “Therefore it is said otherwise that the end of moral science, as also of prudence, is the act of moral virtue”; and it is set out in the *Summa*. But the Doctor vigorously empties this way of speaking. Then comes the fourth opinion on this question, [introduced] thus: “Therefore it is corrected by others”: this opinion says that a habit is called practical from the end which is practical consideration. This is the opinion of Brother John “Roditor”, in the Prologue to [Book] I, q. 6, art. 2; having briefly rejected it, he concludes the second question.

{#s007-04} He begins the first, at the beginning of which the opinion of [[p. 14|v°]] Henry of Ghent is set out from the *Summa*, holding theology to be speculative and not practical, and distinguishing a twofold act of the will, from the *Summa*, art. 9, q. 2, in the reply to the third argument. And it is set out as grounded in the *Summa* and elsewhere, when he adds: “Again, one can argue according to the mind of this [Doctor] elsewhere”, his *Quodlibet* V, q. 4, and *Quodlibet* VIII, q. 1. Then the Doctor begins to argue against him. And in the first argument, which begins: “Against this, first, I turn the reasoning of these men to the opposite”, etc., he argues from what the opinion’s author said about the end in general and in particular, *Quodlibet* IV, q. 11. Again, in the third argument, which begins: “Further, third, whose love is principally intended”, etc., he takes the minor premiss from the [same] opinion, *Quodlibet* XIII, q. 2. Finally the Doctor replies to Henry’s foundations, first to the authority of those things which seem to conclude to the contradictory; he adduces from what the opinion’s author said elsewhere, *Quodlibet* I, q. 9.

{#s007-05} A second way, or opinion, to the same effect is set out where the text says: “The second way says that although the love of the end may be either right or not right”, etc. This way is Brother William’s, called Varro in England and Garro in France, [Book] I, q. 4. He holds that theology is not practical, because it does not have a contingent object. When this has been refuted, the third way follows, where it is said in the text: “The third way holds that volition is not properly *praxis*”, etc. It belongs to Brother Henry Natalis, a Breton by origin, of the Order of Preachers, by office a defender of theology, *Quodlibet* I, q. 3, art. 4. Hence he concludes that theology is not practical but speculative, together with the two preceding, so that these brethren agree in this common [conclusion]. Therefore [the Doctor] argues first against this one in particular, and second against the three together.

{#s007-06} The fourth way, which in the text begins there, “The fourth way says that theology is affective”, is that of the Devout Doctor, [Book] I, q. 3, and of Brother Giles of Rome, [Book] I; the Doctor draws a distinction in it so that it can be well defended. The fifth way follows: “The fifth way says that theology is contemplative”, which Brother William Varro holds, [Book] I, q. 4. The last opinion the Subtle Doctor excludes, so that it may take on a good sense. These five opinions deny that theology is practical; but the last two do not hold it to be speculative, whereas the three preceding do.

{#s007-07} On the opposite side follow two opinions. The first is that of Godfrey of Fontaines, *Quodlibet* XIII, q. 1: he holds that there is a twofold habit of theology, the one practical, the other speculative; and it begins: “Another doctrine is at variance with the preceding.” The second is that of St Thomas, First Part of the *Summa*, q. 1, art. 4. In his principal solution the Doctor does not approve these two: the first [[p. 15|r° · Q.v · sig. b.i]] he refutes in many ways; the second he does not judge worthy of refutation, namely that one and the same simple habit should be at once practical and speculative. The Doctor, for his part, holds that theology is practical, but with a manifold distinction, so that one may say “yes” and “no”.

{#s007-08} Then, when he comes down to resolving Henry of Ghent’s arguments, after the principal solution of the arguments of the first and second questions, at the place “to that about medicine, someone says”, etc.: this “someone” is the same Henry, *Quodlibet* VI, q. 2, and *Quodlibet* XII, q. 22.

{#s007-09} But that this matter may be better explained, I shall do four things: first, [say] what *praxis* is; second, what the difference is between a practical and a speculative habit; third, whence a habit is called practical; fourth, whether theology is practical or speculative, and this will answer the question.

{#s007-10} To the first it is said that *praxis* is “the act of a power other than the intellect, naturally posterior to intellection, apt to be elicited in conformity with right reason so as to be right”. This definition has three clauses. The first, that *praxis* belongs to a power other than the intellect, that is, the will. The second, “naturally posterior to intellection”: that is, the will presupposes the speculative intellect (*Ethics* VI). The third clause, “apt to be elicited in conformity with right reason so as to be right”, is clear from *Ethics* VI, where it is said that right choice needs right reason in order to be right.

{#s007-11} And I give an example, so that this matter may be better understood. The intellect apprehends simple quiddities, and about them it is only speculative (*Ethics* VI). Then it presents quiddities to the will, for instance florins. And then the will determines itself to will them under the aspect of good; at once the will is called *praxis* by its essence, and it is the principle of the practical act. Then comes the intellect, commanded by the will, inquiring into the means by which those florins may be had; this intellect is then called practical imperfectly. Then it moves the limbs to carry those [means] out; and once they are put into execution, it at once becomes perfectly practical. And so Aristotle says (*De anima* III) that “the intellect becomes practical by extension”, which is to be understood thus: the intellect that is imperfectly practical in the first determination becomes practical by extension, that is, perfectly practical in execution; but the speculative intellect moves nothing (*Ethics* VI, the chapter on *praxis*). By what the limbs are moved, whether by the will or by the practical intellect, Aristotle did not determine; for this is his text in [*Ethics*] VI, c. 2, at the end: “Wherefore choice is either appetitive intellect or intellective appetite, and such a principle is man.” But the holy Doctor says that the appetite is the principle.

{#s007-12} As to the second, it is said that the practical posits [[p. 16|v°]] a twofold aptitudinal relation, namely of priority and of conformity to the object, as you have it in the explanation of the definition. And these relations are positive; but the speculative lacks those relations, and by this they are distinguished.

{#s007-13} As to the third, whether they are distinguished by the end: there are authorities of Aristotle for both sides; thus it is said in *De anima* III that they are distinguished by the end. But this is to be understood thus: the end is twofold, namely the end “by which” and the end “which”. Those authorities which hold that they are distinguished by the object are to be understood of the end “by which”, which is the object; those which hold [that they are distinguished] by the true end are to be understood of the end “which”. And so either way can be sustained and the opinions reconciled.

{#s007-14} As to the fourth, I speak with the Doctor and lay down five conclusions. The first: theology of necessary [truths], in a created intellect, blessed or not blessed, angelic or human, is practical. The reason is that this knowledge has from its object a conformity to *praxis* prior to every act of the will, and this aptitudinally. The second conclusion: theology of contingent [truths] is in itself speculative. And the reason is that such contingents, since they do not exist prior to the determining will, cannot show any determination of *praxis*. The third conclusion: theology of contingents in a created intellect is practical, but *per accidens*. And the second and third conclusions do not contradict each other; for theology of contingents is in itself speculative by reason of its object, but in a created intellect it is practical by reason of the will determining the intellect to knowledge of *praxis*. The fourth conclusion: theology, or knowledge, of contingents in the divine intellect is speculative. The fifth conclusion: knowledge of necessary [truths] in the divine intellect is practical. And the reason given for these two conclusions is that in necessary [truths] there is both showing and ratification; but in contingents, although there is showing, there is not ratification, for the ratifying will must supervene.

{#s007-15} But what shall we say to the definition of *praxis*, where it is said: “*Praxis* belongs to a power other than the intellect”? Some say that it does not hold true in God, but [only] in us. Others say that it is understood [as other] either in act or in aptitude, and so such knowledge [is] in the divine intellect prior to the act of the will; and if it does not belong to another power in act, it does so in aptitude.

{#s007-16} Note, in the last words before the Doctor speaks, there where he says: “To this article I say that practical”, etc., there is an explanation for this matter: that if an act is good by its moral genus, the circumstance comes from the end, as God is to be loved because he is supremely good; but if the act is not good by its moral genus, the circumstance will come from the object, as giving alms is good in itself and has its goodness from the object and not from the end, because I can give for the love of God, or so as to be seen by the people. And weigh, [[p. 17|r° · Di.i]] for this matter, this notable [point].

{#s007-17} Note, before the reply to the first [argument], where it is said: “I reply: faith is not a habit”, etc., where you have [the rule] that if some predicate in the first or second mode [of *per se* predication] belongs to something, its opposite belongs to it neither *per se* nor *per accidens*.

{#s007-18} To the argument at the beginning I say that by “astrology” Boethius means metaphysics.
