{#s046-h .heading} [[p. 76|v° · Di.xiii. Q.i]] [Thirteenth distinction.]

{#s046-01} On the thirteenth distinction it is asked whether the production of the Holy Spirit is a generation, or is distinguished from it. It is argued that it is: generation and motion are distinguished by their terms (*Physics* 5); but there is one term in the Son and the Holy Spirit, namely one substance; therefore there is one motion; and so spiration will not be distinguished from generation. To the contrary is Augustine, *On the Trinity* 5, chapter 17: the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son not as born but as given.

{#s046-02} In this question various opinions are set out. The first is that of Giles, in the present distinction, article 2, and of Brother William Varro, in the present distinction; and these hold that the productions are distinguished by their terms, which are the persons produced. He refutes it. The second opinion is that of Thomas, First Part of the *Summa*, question 35, article 2, in the solution of the last argument, which holds that these productions are distinguished because one production is from one person and the other from two. He refutes it. The third opinion is that of Godfrey of Fontaines, *Quodlibet* 3, question 3, that these productions are distinguished by the notions of prior and posterior, since one is together with the other and with its opposite. The fourth opinion is again that of Thomas, First Part, question 27, article 4, in the solution of the first argument, saying that these two productions are distinguished by the notions of prior and posterior. The fifth opinion is that of Henry, in the *Summa*, article 27, question 7, that these emanations are distinguished by the formal elicitive principles, which are held to be the notions of the acting persons. The sixth opinion is that of the same Henry, that they are distinguished by principles distinct in notion, namely by nature and will (*Quodlibet* 5, question 1). The seventh opinion is that of Brother Adam, book 1, distinction 6, question 1, article 2, that the essence, under one wholly undistinguished notion, is the principle of these productions. All of these the Doctor refutes.

{#s046-03} But coming to the question, it is said that the production of the Holy Spirit is not a generation; rather, it is distinguished from it. For according to Augustine, *On the Trinity* 5, chapter 17, the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son as given; but the Son, because he is generated, proceeds as born. But as to the way in which generation and spiration are distinguished, he sets out seven ways. Setting these ways aside, he says to the question that the productions are distinguished by themselves formally. For generation is by itself formally generation; spiration is formally by itself spiration. And from their formal notions it is impossible for generation to be spiration, with anything else whatever set aside, *per impossibile*; so that one need not ask by what [[p. 77|r° · Di.xiiii.et.xv. Q.i]] they are distinguished, except that formally the whole notion of the one is not wholly the same as the formal notion of the other. And this appears from Augustine, *On the Trinity* 15, or chapter 17, where he says: “you will see how, or how far, the birth of the Word differs from the procession of the one given.” And a little further on: that the will proceeds from knowledge, yet is not the image of knowledge; rather, in this matter there is a certain intelligible difference, entire, between procession and birth, etc. There he means to conclude the distinction of the proceeding persons from the distinction of the processions. The same, *On the Trinity* 5, chapter 17, asks why the Holy Spirit is not a son, since he too goes forth from the Father; and he solves it by saying that he is not properly called a son, because he is not born either: for he goes forth from the Father not as born but as given. There, then, he gives the reason of the question about the distinction of the persons through their distinct emanations.

{#s046-04} And if you say, “The emanations are not from themselves; therefore they are not distinguished by themselves,” I reply that specific differences are not from themselves effectively, and yet they are by themselves primarily and formally distinct, although they are effectively distinguished by the same thing from which they effectively are. Some posit three conclusions. The first is that these productions are distinguished by themselves, quidditatively and formally. This is clear because they are multiplied and are of another notion. The second conclusion is that they are distinguished argumentatively from their terms. This is clear because they become known from their terms. The third conclusion is that they are distinguished elicitively from their principles, which are memory and will. This is clear, since one is from memory, the other from will.

{#s046-05} Note, before the seventh way, where he says that relation does not imply perfection, since then some person in God would not have every perfection. Note, before his own opinion, that some thing is infinite in itself and from itself, as the Father in God; some thing is infinite in itself but not from itself, as the Son and the Holy Spirit; some thing is of itself and from itself finite, as a creature. To the argument at the beginning I say that “term” is taken in two ways: in one way substantially, in another way hypostatically. And so I say that there is one term in the first way, but not in the second; or that the first terms are of the same notion, but not adequately.
