{#s035-h .heading} [Third question.]

{#s035-01} [[p. 57|r° · Di.viii. Q.iii]] The third question of the eighth distinction, where it is asked, granted that God is simple, whether God, or anything said of God, is formally in a genus. It seems that God is in a genus: for Avicenna, *Metaphysics* II, chapter 1, says that being in a subject and being not in a subject have no middle term; and by “being not in a subject” substance is meant; but God is not in a subject; therefore [he is] substance. To the contrary is Augustine, who says that those predicates of the dialectical art are removed from God.

{#s035-02} At the beginning the opinion of Henry is set out, in the *Summa*, article 22, questions 1, 2 and 3: that no concept is common to God and the creature. And further on the Doctor answers an argument against him, where the text has: “The reply of some is that ratified being makes knowledge of itself etc.”; this is Henry, *Quodlibet* X, question 7. Again, second, he argues against the Solemn Doctor with the argument touched on above, distinction 3, question 1: when the intellect is certain about one concept and doubtful about two, etc. And he then says that this argument is answered in three ways. First, that one and the same concept is certain and doubtful: as the concept of Socrates and of Plato is doubtful, and the concept of some man is doubtful, and yet [it is] the same; so he replies. Second, it is answered from Henry, *Summa*, article 22, as before, that the concepts of being in God and in the creature are two closely related [concepts], and yet, because they are closely related, they seem one; and here [the intellect] is certain in something doubtful, that is, [certain] in [the concept taken] confusedly, while there is doubt about them conceived distinctly. Third, it is answered by the argument of Brother John of Rodington, Book I, distinction 2, question 6: that there is certainty about both [concepts], distinct, that is, disjunctively, but doubt about each separately or on its own.

{#s035-03} When, however, he says, “There is another opinion at the other extreme etc.”, [the one] that puts God in a genus, this is the opinion of Albert of Saxony and of Brother Robert Holcot, Book I, question 5. And when, much further on, he says, “Some prove that God is not in a genus”, this is Thomas, *Summa*, first part, question 3, article 5, in the principal solution.

{#s035-04} But coming to the question, it is said that neither is God in a genus nor anything said of God. And if at times authorities of Boethius seem [to tell] against this, chiefly that two genera are posited in God, namely substance and relation, it is said that there is no such [statement] in that little book; and were there one, it would be understood [as] two modes of predicating. But that God is not in a genus is plain from Augustine, *On the Trinity* VII, chapter 8: God is called substance improperly, since “substance” properly is said from “standing under”, that is, what stands under accidents, which does not belong to God. Again, Avicenna, *Metaphysics* VIII, chapter 10: God is not in a genus, because a genus is a part; but God is simple, having no part.

{#s035-05} He also proves it by two arguments. First, by infinity: [[p. 58|v° · Di.viii. Q.iiii]] for if he were put in a genus, that genus would be potential with respect to God, from whom the difference would be taken; [but] nothing infinite is potential with respect to another; therefore he is not put in a genus. Or otherwise: every genus is finite; if that genus were posited in God, the finite would be in God, which is unfitting. Second, from necessary being: for that genus either is necessary being or not. If it is, then it is determinate, since all necessary being is determinate; and if determinate, it is no longer a genus. But if it is not necessary being, then God would be constituted from what is not necessary being, which is unfitting. It was also said in the question: “or [whether] anything said of God is in a genus”. And it is said to this that nothing of the kind is in God: for, as I said, the things that are in a genus are limited, whereas those in God are not limited; therefore what is said of God is not formally in a genus.

{#s035-06} Note, in the middle of the question, where it says “I treat this argument somewhat”, that there you will find how some differences are taken from distinct things, some from distinct realities; or that genus and difference are sometimes taken from distinct things, as in things having several forms: for “animal” is taken from the sensitive soul, and “rational” from the intellective soul. Sometimes they are taken from one thing, but from distinct realities, as in a whiteness having ten degrees: from the ninth and the preceding ones the notion of the genus is had, from the tenth the notion of the difference.

{#s035-07} Second, note that he draws a consequence: from the highest in the inferior the highest in the superior never follows, unless that inferior is the noblest thing contained under that superior. Thus it does not follow, “the most perfect ass, therefore the most perfect animal”; but it does follow (for the sake of the example) “the most perfect man, therefore the most perfect animal”. He also says that it is not of the notion of a most general genus that it has several species, but that it has no genus over it. To the argument at the beginning I say that Avicenna means [it] of limited things.
