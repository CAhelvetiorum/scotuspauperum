{#s019-h .heading} [Fourth question.]

{#s019-01} The fourth question of the third distinction, which asks whether any certain and unalloyed truth can be known naturally by the intellect of the wayfarer without a special illumination of the uncreated light. It seems not, from [book IX] *On the Trinity*, ch. 6 and 15: “We behold the inviolable truth, from which we may define, as perfectly as we can…” To the contrary, Romans 1: “these invisible things are known through the visible.”

{#s019-02} Immediately after the arguments, at the foot of the question, the opinion of Henry in the *Summa*, article 1, is set out. He holds that being can be known under the aspect of being but not under the aspect of truth. In the second article, while answering the third [argument], there is the opinion of Henry, who denies all intelligibility of truth, *Quodlibet* V q. 14. In the fourth article the opinion of Henry in the *Summa* is touched upon; [[p. 34|v° · Di.iii. Q.iiii]] whence the Doctor next says: “I say therefore to the question that because of the words etc.” And here the fifth article begins, and it ends at: “From what has been said, the answer to all the authorities of Augustine to the contrary is clear.” The sixth article, however, is not found in the books in common use, and so I have not included it.

{#s019-03} But coming to the question, it is said with Augustine that infallible truths are seen, understood and known in the eternal rules. Here it should be noted that the word ‘in’ can be taken objectively, and that in four ways: either as in the proximate object; or as in what contains the proximate object; or as in that by virtue of which the proximate object moves; or as in the remote object. On the first: all intelligibles have intelligible being through the act of the divine intellect, and in them all the truths about them shine forth. On the second: the divine intellect contains these truths like a book, as Augustine says in *On the Trinity* [XIV, not] IX, that these reasons are written in the book of the eternal light, that is, in the divine intellect insofar as it contains these truths. And although that book is not seen, the quiddities written in that first book are seen. To that extent our intellect can be said to see truths in the eternal light, that is, in that first book, as in what contains the object. This is the second mode, which is to contain truths like a book, inasmuch as it produces the secondary objects in intelligible being. On the third, it is said that we see in the eternal light as in the cause of the object in itself: namely, that it produces the secondary objects in intelligible being, and that it is that by virtue of which the secondary objects, once produced, actually move the intellect. On the fourth, it is said that those things, as the secondary object of the divine intellect, have being only in a qualified sense. But a true, real operation does not belong to anything that is a being merely in a qualified sense by virtue of itself; and if it ever belongs to it, it must be by virtue of something else to which being belongs absolutely. Therefore it does not belong to these secondary objects to move the intellect of themselves, except by virtue of the being of the divine intellect, which is being absolutely, and through which they have being in a qualified sense.

{#s019-04} But to the question it is said that those truths can be known naturally through these sensible things, according to Romans 1, and so no special illumination is required. This is clear from Augustine, *On the Trinity* IV, ch. 35, where he speaks of the unbelieving philosophers: “Some of them,” he says, “were able to raise the eye of the mind beyond every creature and to attain in part the light of unchangeable truth, and they deride many Christians, who live by faith alone, as not yet able to reach it.” He therefore means that Christians do not see what they believe in the eternal rules, whereas the philosophers see many necessary truths in them.

{#s019-05} But what is to be said to the argument from *On the Trinity* XII, ch. [1]4, that “it belongs to few to reach with the eye of the mind [[p. 35|r° · Di.iii. Q.v · sig. c.ii]] the intelligible reasons,” and from the *Eighty-Three Questions*, q. 46, that “only pure souls attain them”? It is said that this purity is not to be understood as freedom from vices, for in *On the Trinity* XIV he says that the unjust man sees in the eternal rules what it is just to do. Rather, this purity is to be understood as raising the intellect to truths as they shine forth in themselves, and not as they shine forth in the phantasm.

{#s019-06} But what is to be said to Augustine on the requirement of light, who seems to say that uncreated light is required in our soul for the knowledge of infallible truths? It is said that Augustine spoke by way of inquiry and not of determination. Note for this matter that the proximate object for us, with respect to truths, is the secondary objects. To the argument at the beginning it is said that these truths are known only through these sensible things.
