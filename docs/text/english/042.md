{#s042-h .heading} [Twelfth distinction.]

{#s042-01} On the twelfth distinction it is asked whether the Father and the Son spirate the Holy Spirit inasmuch as they are altogether one, or inasmuch as they are in some way distinct. It seems not [as one]: because the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son as from begetter and begotten; but as such they are distinct; therefore etc. To the contrary, according to Augustine, the three are one principle of the creature; therefore the two are one principle of the Holy Spirit.

{#s042-02} In this question, where the doubt is raised whether the Father and the Son spirate the Holy Spirit by the will rather as it is one or as it is concordant, there is first set out the opinion of Henry in the *Summa*, article 54. Afterwards the Subtle Doctor argues against him; and in the last argument he says: “Further, against the one holding this opinion, since he holds that the Holy Spirit could be distinguished from the Son even if he did not proceed from him.” Henry has this in *Quodlibet* 5, question 9.

{#s042-03} But coming to the question, four articles are to be set out here. First, whether the Father and the Son are one principle of the Holy Spirit. Second, whether they are his principle inasmuch as they are one or inasmuch as they are distinct. Third, whether they are his principle uniformly. [[p. 70|v° · Di.xii. Q.i]] Fourth, whether active spiration terminates at its opposite.

{#s042-04} But first I shall set out some necessary points. The first is that the Father and the Son are not one principle *which* of the Holy Spirit; for it would follow that both were one person, which is false, since one principle *which* is with respect to one person. The second is that the Father and the Son are not one principle *by which* of the Holy Spirit; for it would follow that the fruitful will of the Father and of the Son would be formally the same as the Father and the Son, which is not so. The third is that the Father and the Son are one principle essentially. And the reason is that both are one essentially, or the same; and so, if they concur as one principle, they concur as one essentially.

{#s042-05} Then to the first article the answer is affirmative. The reason is that the Father, in begetting the Son, communicates to him every absolute perfection not incompatible with him; and so he communicates to him the fruitful will, which is the principle of the Holy Spirit. Since, then, there is the same fruitful will in the Father and the Son, it follows that the Father and the Son are one principle of the Holy Spirit. This is confirmed by Augustine, *On the Trinity* 5, who says: the Father and the Son are one principle of the Holy Spirit, as the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are one principle of the creature. But creatures are produced by them inasmuch as they are one; therefore etc. It is confirmed by what is had in the *Extra*, [title] *On the Catholic Faith*, in the *Sext*, where it is said: “With faithful and devout profession we confess that the Holy Spirit proceeds eternally from the Father and the Son, not as from two principles but as from one principle, and by one spiration.”

{#s042-06} As to the second, there are three ways of speaking. The first is Thomas's, First Part, question 36, article 4, in the solution of the first argument: he says that, as to the spirative power, the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son inasmuch as they are one; but as to the supposits producing the Holy Spirit, he proceeds from the Father and the Son inasmuch as they are distinct. Another way of speaking is that the Father and the Son produce the Holy Spirit inasmuch as they are altogether one. The reason is that the word “inasmuch” reduplicates the formal cause; but the cause, that is, the will, is the same in the Father and the Son with respect to the Holy Spirit; therefore they produce him inasmuch as they are one. Another way of speaking is that the act of spirating can be considered precisely in itself; and thus they produce the Holy Spirit neither inasmuch as they are one nor inasmuch as they are distinct, but it abstracts from both, as Avicenna, *Metaphysics* 5, puts it of horseness, which abstracts from one and many. In another way the act of spirating can [[p. 71|r° · Di.xii. Q.i]] be considered as simple and perfect; and because this comes from the unity of the principle, they therefore produce inasmuch as they are one.

{#s042-07} As to the third, I say that the act of spirating in the present case can be considered in three ways: either in itself, or as it is a term, or as it is compared to the acting supposits. In the first two ways the Father and the Son spirate the Holy Spirit uniformly; but in the third way not, because the Father has from himself that he spirates, whereas the Son has it from the Father.

{#s042-08} As to the fourth: whether active spiration terminates at its opposite, namely at passive spiration. And it is the same to ask whether a relation terminates at its correlative. And he says that relation, neither in God nor in creatures, terminates at its opposite, but at its foundation; just as paternity does not terminate at filiation but at the foundation of filiation. From this the following conclusions follow. The first is that active generation in God terminates at the divine essence. The second conclusion is that active spiration terminates at the same essence. The third conclusion is that filiation terminates at the fruitful memory. The fourth conclusion is that passive spiration terminates at the fruitful will. You will say that Aristotle says that paternity terminates at filiation. It is answered that this is to be understood [thus]: filiation terminates at the foundation of the father in which paternity is founded, namely at the power of begetting.

{#s042-09} Note that someone may say: the Father and the Son love each other; but in that love the Holy Spirit is produced; therefore he will be produced first, before he is produced. And you could argue in the same way about the Son. And therefore it is said that the Father, when he understood the Son, did not produce him, but when he had fruitful memory regarding him; or, according to the Doctor in the first *Quodlibet*, he did not produce him by understanding but by speaking. So it is said of the Holy Spirit that he is not produced by the first volition between them, but the Holy Spirit is produced by the fruitful will between them: not that it is another act of will, but the same act made fruitful. But someone might say: is the will equally perfect in the Father alone as in the Father and the Son? I say yes. Then why does the Father alone not produce the Holy Spirit? I say that in the Father it is equally perfect, and he can produce the Holy Spirit by remote power but not by proximate power. For this [proximate power] is in the two, namely the fruitfulness of ⟨requited⟩ love, or mutual will. To the argument at the beginning I say that they are distinct among themselves, but not with respect to the term or with respect to the production of the term.
