{#s006-01} The fourth question of the Prologue, where three questions are posed. First, whether theology is a science. Second, whether it stands to another science in the relation of subalternating or subalternated. Third, whether contingent things belong to the theologian as necessary ones do.

{#s006-02} It seems not, by Augustine, *De Trinitate* XV, c. 16, where he says: “Perhaps our thoughts will not be fleeting, going and returning from one thing to another”; and so the intellect of the blessed will not reason discursively, and discursive reasoning is required for science. To the contrary: for although in the saints there is no discursus in time, there is nevertheless a discursus of nature, which suffices for science.

{#s006-03} [[p. 12|v°]] This question the Doctor passes over cursorily, and does not set out opinions on it. But coming to the first question, there is a certain opinion which says that theology is a science, not indeed in the light of faith nor in the light of glory, but in a certain intermediate light, in the way in which we speak of the morning light, to which the light of faith corresponds, and the light of midday, to which the light of glory corresponds, and an intermediate light which advances continually from morning to midday. Scotus rejects it, saying that no such intermediate light is to be found. And this is found in the *reportationes*.

{#s006-04} There is another opinion, Ware’s, [Book] I, q. 3, that theology can be considered in two ways, namely in itself or in relation to the one who knows it. In the first way it is a science; in the second way, as it is in the intellect of the wayfarer, since it does not have more evident principles, it ought not to be called a science. It is refuted.

{#s006-05} The Doctor’s opinion is that, taking science as “to know” is defined in *Posterior Analytics* I, it has four conditions: first, that it is certain knowledge of what is true; second, of what is necessary; third, having its evidence from something necessary and evident beforehand; fourth, applied to what is known through syllogistic discursus.

{#s006-06} The first is proved from *Ethics* VI, where it is said: “About science one must be certified, and not follow likenesses.” The second condition is had from *Metaphysics* VI: of particular and mutable things there is no science. The third condition is clear from *Posterior Analytics* I: “We know the principles inasmuch as we know the terms”; and by this science is distinguished from the understanding of principles (*Ethics* VI). The fourth condition does not belong *per se* to the notion of science, nor does it belong to science except in that intellect which happens to reason discursively and to proceed from the known to the unknown.

{#s006-07} From these [premisses], to the matter in hand: as regards the first three conditions, theology in itself, and in the divine intellect and that of the blessed, is a true science. But concerning the fourth condition some have doubts. But the Doctor says that it is a science: and although there is no temporal discursus there, there is nevertheless a discursus in the order of nature, and that suffices for science. And if any authorities of Augustine are found that seem to reject discursus, they are to be understood of temporal discursus.

{#s006-08} As to the second question, whether our theology is subalternated to the theology of the blessed and of God, and to metaphysics. On this question there is the opinion of Thomas, First Part, q. 1, art. 2, who says that our theology is subalternated to the science of God and of the blessed. But the Doctor refutes it: for a subalternating and a subalternated science are not about the same conclusions; but this theology, namely ours, and that of God and the blessed, are [about the same conclusions]; therefore [ours] is not subalternated. Nor is it subalternated to metaphysics: for subalternating and subalternated [sciences] must belong to different categories, as number and sound in music, [[p. 13|r° · Q.iiiii]] for number is in the category of quantity, sound in that of quality. Not so with being and with God: for if they were placed in a category, they would be placed not in distinct categories but in the same one.

{#s006-09} As to the third question, whether contingent things belong to the theologian as necessary ones do, it is said that they do. For although the theologian considers contingent things, he does so not in a contingent but in a necessary manner; and so he has science of them not through contingent but through necessary reasons, and this suffices for science: just as we say in philosophy that there is science of things generable and corruptible, not indeed through particular and corruptible propositions, but through universal and incorruptible ones.

{#s006-10} To the argument: the authority of Augustine is not compelling, because he speaks doubtfully.
