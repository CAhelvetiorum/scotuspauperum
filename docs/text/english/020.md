{#s020-h .heading} [Fifth question.]

{#s020-01} The fifth question of the third distinction, which asks whether there is a trace of the Trinity in the creature. It seems not: through a trace one can track down that of which it is the trace; therefore through the creature the Trinity could be tracked down naturally, which is false, because this exceeds natural reason. To the contrary is Augustine, *On the Trinity* VI, last chapter: “We must, beholding the Creator through the things that are made, understand the Trinity, of which a trace appears in the creature in the way that is fitting.”

{#s020-02} In this question there are three articles. In the first article is set out the opinion of Thomas, *On the First Book* [of the *Sentences*], d. 3, article 2: namely, that a trace is an impression left behind by the passage of something over the empty or the full, representing it in part, because [it represents] after the manner of the species. As to the second article, on the relation of the creature to God, in which relation the formal aspect of a trace consists, the opinion of Henry is set out, *Quodlibet* IX qq. 1 and 2, on the twofold relation. As to the third article, on the foundations of the trace, there is the opinion of Henry, *Quodlibet* III q. 9, *Quodlibet* V q. 2, *Quodlibet* IV qq. 7 and 8, and *Quodlibet* II q. 3. He says that the relation of the trace in the creature is not founded on the ‘ratitude’ of the thing, but on its ‘aliquity’ alone.

{#s020-03} But coming to the Doctor’s opinion, three things must be seen. First, what a trace is, and how it is distinguished from an image. Second, in what it exists, and with respect to what in God the formal aspect of a trace in the creature lies. Third, on what the relation of the trace, that is, of the creature to God, whose trace it is called, is founded.

{#s020-04} As to the first, it is said that a trace is a likeness of a part, representing the whole inferentially, as from the shape of an ox’s hoof in the dust we infer that an ox has passed by. [[p. 36|v° · Di.iii. Q.vi]] ‘Inferentially’ is put in because, if the foot were severed, it would not properly represent the ox. An image, by contrast, is a likeness representing the whole, as an image of Jupiter represents Jupiter.

{#s020-05} As to the second, it is said following Augustine, *On the Trinity*, last [chapter of book VI], [who] says of creatures: “All these things that have been made by divine art show in themselves a certain unity, form and order.” The unity of the creature represents the highest unity of the first principle, from which is the origin. And as to this he says: “In that Trinity is the highest origin of all things.” Form [*species*] in the creature represents the highest beauty. Order, or operation, in the creature represents the most perfect operation in God, for beatitude is the highest love. And the Doctor disagrees with the others in this article, for he holds that these are founded primarily in absolutes; the others do not.

{#s020-06} As to the third, it is said that with respect to [things] appropriated by the soul, we appropriate the unity of the creature with its relation, and its form and order, to God: namely, unity to the Father, form to the Son, order and love to the Holy Spirit.

{#s020-07} But someone might ask how the formal aspect of a trace, which is a likeness of a part, is taken in the divine, since in the divine there is no part. I answer: the Trinity is a certain numerable whole, at least in the concept of our intellect, and a person there is as it were a part of this whole. So what is appropriated to a person, insofar as it is appropriated, is there as it were a part of the same whole, because it is taken for him to whom it is appropriated. And not only this: what is appropriable, even if it is not taken as appropriated, is still there as it were a part of the same. To the argument at the beginning it is said that through a trace that of which it is the trace is tracked down, but not distinctly: indistinctly and inferentially.
