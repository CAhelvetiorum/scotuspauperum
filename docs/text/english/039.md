{#s039-h .heading} [Tenth distinction.]

{#s039-01} On the tenth distinction it is asked whether the Holy Spirit is produced by way of will. It seems not. Augustine, *On the Trinity* 15, says: the Holy Spirit proceeds necessarily; therefore not by way of will, etc. To the contrary, the same Augustine says that the Holy Spirit proceeds by way of love.

{#s039-02} In resolving the first doubt, there is set out first the opinion of the Solemn Doctor in the *Summa*, article 60, question 1, who draws a distinction concerning nature. As to the second doubt, the same doctor replies by distinguishing a twofold action of the will. Then the Doctor begins to argue against him: in the second argument he says that the Solemn Doctor holds that the notional acts are founded on the essential acts, and this in *Quodlibet* 6, question 1. And in the last argument he says that he holds that will and nature are distinguished truly only according to reason; on this, *Quodlibet* 5, question 1. When, third, he replies to the doubt, the doubt is the one to which Henry replies in *Quodlibet* 6, question 1.

{#s039-03} But in this question we shall consider four things. First, whether there is will in God. Second, whether there is will in God under the aspect of a productive principle. Third, whether the Holy Spirit is produced by an act of will. Fourth, some doubts will be removed.

{#s039-04} To the first I say that there is will in God, which is proved thus. Beatitude consists ⟨in⟩ the act of intellect and will (*Ethics* 10); but in God there is perfect beatitude; therefore in him there is an act of will, and consequently will. The Holy Doctor, in the First Part, question 19, article 1, proves it thus. As sensitive appetite follows sensitive cognition, so intellective appetite follows intellective cognition. But wherever there is sense there is sensitive appetite; therefore wherever there is intellect there is intellective appetite, or will. But in God there is intellect; therefore will. This is had from Augustine, *On the Trinity* 5, chapter 17: “The Holy Spirit went forth from the Father and the Son, not as one born but as one given.” To go forth by way of something given, or of a gift, belongs to what is produced by way of will, since it belongs to will, namely, to give or bestow out of its freedom.

{#s039-05} To the second, it is proved that it is [in God] under the aspect of a productive principle. For productive principles, since of themselves they imply no imperfection, are reduced to some one, or to some [[p. 65|r° · Di.x. Q.i]] perfect ones, in as great a fewness as they can be reduced to. They cannot all be reduced to one productive or active principle, because that single one would have the determinate mode of acting of one of the two, namely of nature or of will, since between these modes of producing there is no middle mode. Therefore they cannot be reduced to a greater fewness than to the duality of a productive principle by way of nature and by way of will. And yet those things at which, as at perfect ones, this whole reduction of principles comes to rest are simply perfect. And both these principles, each under its proper aspect, are posited in God.

{#s039-06} But granted that will is the productive principle of the Holy Spirit, [it is asked] whether the essence concurs together with it, and if so, how they stand to each other. To this I state four conclusions. First, that the divine essence is a partial principle, concurring under the aspect of object. And this is clear, because nothing contains the term formally or virtually except the essence. Again, because this production must be univocal, and that univocity must come about in the essence. The second conclusion is that the will is the principal principle, concurring under the aspect of a power; for it is impossible that the act of spirating should exist without the will concurring, just as the act of speaking [cannot] without the intellect concurring, since to speak is to produce something by way of intellect. The third conclusion is that these two are one total principle. This is clear, because each concurs and neither suffices; and it is impossible that there should be, with respect to the same product, two total causes or two total principles of the same kind. The fourth conclusion is that the essence concurs more principally than that which concurs as a particular principle; for the essence concurs as a universal principle, the will as a particular principle.

{#s039-07} And note here that some have wished to say that it is not Scotus's intention to posit a begotten knowledge in God, that is, that there is no begotten act of understanding in God, nor [a begotten] act of willing; and that he posits these acts not as elicited but as innate.

{#s039-08} As to the third, it is said that it is so, on Augustine's testimony (*On the Trinity* 15), [and] by reason thus. Whatever is, by its formal notion, a productive principle can, wherever it exists without imperfection, produce, before it is understood to have an adequate product. And if it is necessary being, it produces necessarily. But in God there is a fruitful will under the aspect of a productive principle (from the second article), and it is necessary being. Therefore what has it produces necessarily in this manner: just as a finite will having a finite and adequate lovable object produces a finite love, so an infinite will, such as the divine will is, having an infinite lovable object, produces an infinite adequate love. But the infinite is subsistent of itself; and it has a love [[p. 66|v° · Di.x. Q.i]] infinite and subsistent of itself: I mean the Holy Spirit.

{#s039-09} As to the fourth, doubts occur. The first is how the divine will is the principle of communicating the nature, since this does not belong to a created will. It is answered in two ways. In one way, according to Henry, “nature” is taken in four ways. In one way, nature as it is the essence in which the three persons exist. In a second way, nature is the natural active principle to which it belongs to assimilate; and thus it is called the essential contracted to the notional. In a third way, “nature” is said of anything existing naturally in nature taken in the first way. In a fourth way, “nature” is said of an incommunicable necessity ordered to some act. To the point at issue he says that intellect and will are principles of communicating the divine nature, not inasmuch as they are intellect and will, but inasmuch as they are nature in the third way, through the co-assistance of nature taken in the first way. The Doctor says otherwise: that the will can be the principle of communicating the nature, not will as taken in common for created and uncreated will, but will as infinite; for infinity is the proper mode of the divine will.

{#s039-10} The second doubt is whether the production of the Holy Spirit is necessary, and how this necessity stands with freedom. To the first it is said that the production is necessary, which is clear thus. To what is supremely produced belongs the noblest production; but necessary production is the noblest; therefore etc. Again, a necessary being, if it is produced, is produced necessarily; but the Holy Spirit is a necessary being; therefore he is produced necessarily. But as to what that necessity is to be called, it is said: not a necessity of coercion but of natural determination, or, according to some, of requisite perfection.

{#s039-11} To the second, how it stands with freedom, it is answered in different ways by different people. Some say that the will can be considered in two ways: in one way as nature, and thus it produces necessarily; in another way inasmuch as it is free, and thus it produces contingently, and thus it is not the principle of the Holy Spirit. Others say that, although there is necessity as the act tends to its object, there is no necessity as the act is elicited by the power. Others say that the will, as it elicits the act, is free; but as it terminates or fixes itself in the act, it is thus necessary, etc. The Doctor says that there is freedom and necessity there; for just as nature has its own necessity and its own contingency, inasmuch as it can fail, so freedom has its necessity and its contingency; and the necessity of the will stands well with freedom. But someone might say that this does not settle the question. Therefore a certain doctor says that an infinite will, having present such an object as is [[p. 67|r° · Di.xi. Q.i · sig. e.2]] necessarily to be loved, stands necessarily as regards the act but freely as regards election; and thus it will be necessary and free. Or it is called free with the freedom of complacency. To the argument at the beginning I say that Augustine takes “necessarily” for the person, not for the principle by which.
