{#s064-h .heading} [Twenty-sixth distinction.]

{#s064-01} On the twenty-sixth distinction it is asked whether the divine persons are constituted in personal being by the relations of origin, that is, by the proper relations. It seems not, [for] according to Richard, in the definition of person ‘substance’ is placed, which seems to signify something absolute. To the contrary: according to Boethius, relation multiplies the Trinity. Likewise Augustine: whatever is [said] with respect to itself is common to all.

{#s064-02} At the beginning is set out the opinion of Praepositinus, who says that the divine persons are distinguished by their whole selves. Against which the Doctor argues with his own arguments; and at the end he says: ‘It is also argued otherwise against him: because if the Father by his whole self, etc.’; this is the argument of Henry the Preacher. After this is set out the common opinion, which says that the divine persons are constituted by relations; this is followed by St Thomas, *Summa*, first part, q. ⟨39⟩, art. 2. In opposition, in that place, there is a third opinion, agreeing with the reasons and authorities adduced. Against the second is set out the opinion which says that the divine persons are constituted by absolutes, which is followed by Brother John of Ripa in his [commentary on the] first [book] and in Quodlibets 26 and 25. And there, lest it seem new, it is strengthened by the authority of the same ancient doctor, namely St John Bonaventure, so that John may be helped by John, in his [commentary on the] first [book], d. 25, q. 1.

{#s064-03} But coming to the question: there was the opinion of the Master that the divine persons are constituted and distinguished by themselves, and so there is no need to ask how they are constituted and distinguished. It is refuted. There is another opinion, that the persons are constituted by intrinsic absolute modes, which modes are unknown to us; but the persons of the Trinity are determined for us by the relative properties, so that, as it were *a priori*, we know the persons in some way through these relations. It is refuted. The opinion of the Doctor, and the true one, is that the persons are constituted by the relative properties. And he proves it through the authorities of the saints: first by the authority of [[p. 102|v° · Di.xxvi. Q.ii]]Dionysius, *On the Divine Names*, where he says thus: ‘We must not dare, nor think, to say anything of that supreme and ineffable Deity except what has been handed down to us through Sacred Scripture.’ But through Sacred Scripture we nowhere have it that the persons in God are constituted by absolutes, but rather by the relative properties; for it is said in the canonical [epistle] of John: ‘There are three that bear witness in heaven, namely the Father, the Word and the Holy Spirit.’ The second is from Boethius, *De Trinitate*: ‘The essence holds the unity, relation multiplies the Trinity.’ The third is from Anselm, *On the Procession of the Holy Spirit*, where he says that in God there is supreme unity where an opposed relation does not stand in the way. By reason, thus: either a person is constituted by something absolute or by something relative. If by something relative, I have my thesis. If by something absolute, then either by the same [absolute] in which it agrees with another person, or by another. Not by the same, since by that it would not be distinguished from the other person; therefore by another. But an absolute added to an absolute makes a composition; and so there would be composition in God, which is false.

{#s064-04} And if it be asked how the same nature can be in several *supposita* really distinct, it is said that this is on account of the infinity of the essence, and because the *supposita* are relative; for the same unlimited thing can be the foundation of several opposed relatives.

{#s064-05} But someone might ask how many conditions are required for a relation to constitute a person. It is said: three. The first, that the relation be *insistens*, that is, existing within, and not *assistens*, that is, concomitant or present. The second, that the property itself be subsistent and not inherent. This is clear, for what inheres is posterior, and the posterior does not constitute the prior. The third, that that property be proper and not common. This is clear, for the formal *ratio* of each thing is proper to that whose it is; but what is common cannot in this way be proper.

{#s064-06} To the argument at the beginning it is said that, although ‘substance’ is placed in the definition of person, that is a notification and not a definition.
