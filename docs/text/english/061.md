{#s061-h .heading} [Twenty-third distinction.]

{#s061-01} On the twenty-third distinction it is asked whether ‘person’, inasmuch as it signifies something common to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, signifies principally something of second intention. It seems so: for ‘individual’ is a name of second intention; therefore so is ‘person’. The consequence holds, since they have the same notion. To the contrary is Richard, *On the Trinity* 4.

{#s061-02} At the beginning of the solution is set down the opinion of Giles, who says that this name ‘person’ signifies only a second intention. Again, it is the view of Ware, book 1, that ‘person’ is a name of second intention.

{#s061-03} But coming to the question, the view of Scotus is that ‘person’ is a name of first intention; and he proves it thus. A second intention is not the term of a real production; but a person is the term of a real production, namely the person of the Son; therefore etc. Again, a second intention is not adored; but a person is adored; therefore etc. Again, the Trinity does not consist in concepts; but the Trinity consists in persons; therefore a person is not a concept or a second intention. Again, a definition expresses what is the being of the thing whose definition it is; but the definition of person expresses a first intention; therefore the name ‘person’ is a name of first intention. The minor is clear from Richard, *On the Trinity* 4, who says that a person is the individual subsistence of an intellectual nature. Again, a second intention signifies a relation of reason; but ‘person’ does not signify a relation of reason. Again, a person is an object of fruition; but no second intention is an object of fruition; therefore a person is not a second intention.

{#s061-04} Some say that ‘person’ can be taken in two ways: either for that on which the name is imposed, and so Scotus holds that it is a first intention; or, in another way, it is taken for the name which is imposed, or from which the name is imposed, and so ‘person’ is a name of second intention. But these men say what a first intention is; to which they adduce certain conclusions. The first conclusion: that a first intention is not an act of understanding; for one intention is predicated of another, but one act of understanding is not predicated of another. The second conclusion: that a first intention is not the quiddity of the object actually understood; for then it would follow that a man would not be an animal except when he was actually understanding. [[p. 98|v° · Di.xxiiii. Q.ii]]The third conclusion: that the quiddity of the object, as it is intelligible in potency, is not a first intention. The fourth conclusion: that a first intention is the quidditative being of each thing, as is had from Avicenna, *Metaphysics* 5, that horseness is only horseness. But a second intention is a certain relation of reason, founded upon the first and fashioned by the act of our intellect. And these men lay down two rules. The first: that whenever something superior is assigned to something inferior and is of its essence, that superior is a first intention. The second rule: that when something superior is assigned to something inferior and is not predicated of all the inferiors, it is a second intention. Concerning which it is also to be noted that these four stand in an order and are essentially ordered, namely person, *suppositum*, singular and individual. For it follows: ‘it is a person, therefore a *suppositum*’, and not conversely, for an ass is a *suppositum* but not a person. And upon *suppositum* follows singular, and not conversely, for a stone is singular but not a *suppositum*. Upon singular follows individual, and not conversely, for whiteness is an individual but not a singular. But Francis says of second intentions that second intentions are aptitudes which belong to the quiddities themselves, or to first intentions, in quidditative being; as the aptitude of being communicated to several individuals, supervening upon humanity, is the *ratio* of species; and so of genus.

{#s061-05} To the argument at the beginning: it is denied that individual and person are of the same *ratio*; rather, they are distinguished and have distinct *rationes*.
