{#s034-h .heading} [Second question.]

{#s034-01} The second question of the eighth distinction, where it is asked whether any creature is simple. It seems that it is simple: for a composite is composed of parts, and those [parts] are not [composed] of others; therefore those parts are simple in themselves. To the contrary is Augustine, *On the Trinity*, chapter 5, [who] says that no creature is simple in itself.

{#s034-02} At the beginning the holy Doctor [Thomas], in the first part of the *Summa*, question 3, article 4, and in question 50, article 2, answering the third and fourth arguments, and in book II of the *Contra Gentiles*, chapter 49, says that every creature is composed of act and potency. The Doctor [Scotus] disproves this.

{#s034-03} But coming to the question, he answers that there is some creature that is simple, that is, not composite; yet no creature is perfectly simple, because it is either composite or composable. I explain this as follows: no creature has entity according to the whole of perfection; rather it lacks some perfection that is apt to belong to entity in itself, and so it is deprived of it, as the mole is called blind because it lacks that, namely sight, which it is apt to have and does not have, at least according to the notion of animal, according to the Philosopher, *Metaphysics* V, chapter on privation. It is composed, therefore, not of positive thing and thing, but of a positive thing and a privative one: not that the privation composes, since the privative is not of the essence of the positive; but we say it is composed in this sense, that with that being there is that privation, that is, a being undetermined to such [a perfection] is composable with another. Moreover, every creature is composable. This is plain of an accident, which is composed with substance; and of substance, both of matter and of form, from *Physics* I. And so it is concluded. And he answers the question that every creature is either composite or composable; and so no creature is simply simple.

{#s034-04} And if it is said that accidents are simple, it is said that this is true insofar as simplicity is opposed to the composition that is of matter and form; not, however, [as opposed] to the composition of genus and difference, which properly should not be called composition but rather constitution; for composition properly is of matter and form, from *Physics* I. To the argument at the beginning I say that every creature is composite or composable; and those parts, even if they are not composite, are composable.
